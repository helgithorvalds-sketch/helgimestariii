# Dyslexia and multimedia learning: evidence for designing narrated explainer videos

Scope note: these notes cover learners with reading dyslexia who use narrated video to study school subjects (science, history, maths, social studies). The goal is subject understanding and memory, not reading instruction. Evidence strength is flagged per item as **[STRONG]**, **[MODERATE]**, **[WEAK/MIXED]**, **[MYTH]** or **[PRACTITIONER]** (craft advice without learning-outcome trials). "Snippet-level" means the figure came from an abstract or search summary and the full text was not opened; verify before quoting in print.

---

## 1. What does dyslexia impair, what is typically intact, and are dyslexic learners "visual learners"?

### Takeaway
Dyslexia is a word-level reading and spelling difficulty (accuracy and/or speed) rooted mainly in phonological processing, usually with weaker verbal short-term/working memory and slower naming; reasoning is not part of the deficit and listening comprehension is far better preserved than reading comprehension (though not perfectly intact on average, and comorbid language or attention problems are common). The claims that dyslexic people are "visual learners" or have superior visual-spatial ability are not supported: meta-analysis shows lower average visuo-spatial scores with wider spread, and matching teaching to "learning styles" has no adequate evidence base. Pictures help dyslexic viewers for the same reason they help everyone (the multimedia principle), not because of a special visual talent.

### Cited Findings

**Definition and core profile [STRONG]**
- The International Dyslexia Association's revised definition (board-approved October 2025; first major revision since 2002) describes dyslexia as "a specific learning disability characterized by difficulties in word reading and/or spelling that involve accuracy, speed, or both and vary depending on the orthography"; reading comprehension problems and reduced reading experience are listed as *secondary consequences* that "can impede growth in language, knowledge, written expression, and overall academic achievement" (snippet-level; IDA page returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch) — [IDA 2025 Dyslexia Definition Project](https://dyslexiaida.org/2025-dyslexia-definition-project/)
- Snowling, Hulme & Nation (2020, *Oxford Review of Education* 46(4), 501–513) propose reserving the term for "a difficulty with decoding and spelling fluency which is evident from the early school years and persistent over time"; "Dyslexia is a dimensional disorder ... with no clear cut-off from poor reading." — [Snowling, Hulme & Nation 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7455053/)
- Same review: "in cognitive terms, dyslexia is caused by problems at the level of phonological representation", which also bring difficulty learning new spoken words, word-retrieval/naming problems and poor verbal short-term memory; but "phonological difficulties are neither necessary nor sufficient to account for dyslexia" (multiple-risk model). — [Snowling, Hulme & Nation 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7455053/)
- Peterson & Pennington (2012, *Lancet* 379, 1997–2007): dyslexia is "difficulties with accurate and/or fluent word recognition and spelling despite adequate instruction and intelligence"; it is "the lower end of a normal distribution of word reading ability"; a common definition "identifies 7% of the population". — [Peterson & Pennington 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465717/)
- Peterson & Pennington: phonological awareness was "the main predictor of reading in every language" (stronger in less consistent orthographies); rapid serial naming contributes independent risk and "correlate[s] highly with nonverbal measures of processing speed", which predict reading fluency; verbal short-term memory makes a smaller contribution. — [Peterson & Pennington 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465717/)
- A meta-analysis of phonological skills reports verbal short-term memory deficits of about d = −0.71 in dyslexic groups versus age-matched controls (snippet-level; Melby-Lervåg, Lyster & Hulme 2012). — [Melby-Lervåg et al. 2012 (PDF copy)](https://eclass-b.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PHYCH108/%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%98%CE%A1%CE%91%20%CE%93%CE%99%CE%91%20%CE%A4%CE%99%CE%A3%20%CE%A0%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%9F%CE%A5%CE%A3%CE%99%CE%91%CE%A3%CE%95%CE%99%CE%A3%20-%20PAPERS%20FOR%20PRESENTATION/2012%20Phonological%20skills%20and%20their%20role%20in%20learning%20to%20read%20A%20meta%20analytic%20review.pdf)
- In a multimedia-learning sample of 11-year-olds (26 dyslexic, 38 typical), the dyslexic group scored much lower on verbal working memory (d = 1.24, p < .001) and marginally lower on visual working memory (d = 0.45, p < .10). — [Knoop-van Campen, Segers & Verhoeven 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6084336)

**What is (relatively) intact [MODERATE]**
- "Dyslexia is defined by difficulties with decoding, while comprehension is typically relatively intact"; "poor comprehenders" show the opposite profile; "language skill is a known protective factor". — [Peterson & Pennington 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465717/)
- Meta-analysis of 76 studies (Georgiou et al. 2022, *Annals of Dyslexia* 72, 204–248): reading comprehension deficit versus chronological-age controls g = 1.43, versus reading-level controls g = 0.64; **listening comprehension** deficit versus age controls only g = 0.43 (re-analysis 0.46, 95% CI 0.17–0.74) and essentially zero versus reading-level controls (0.02, n.s.). The authors read the small listening effect as a sign of broader language weaknesses in some children. (Snippet-level.) — [Georgiou et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11881-021-00244-y)
- So the listening route is roughly three times less impaired than the reading route (g 0.43 vs 1.43), but "intact" overstates it at group level. — [Georgiou et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11881-021-00244-y)
- Children with *dyslexia only* (no additional language difficulty) scored significantly higher on comprehension in all conditions and "derived significantly more benefit in reading comprehension from TTS and the listen only condition" than children with combined reading and language difficulties (Keelor et al. 2023, *Annals of Dyslexia* 73(3), 469–486; snippet-level). — [Keelor et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37119436/)

**Comorbidity: the audience is not homogeneous [STRONG]**
- "About 40% of the children with a reading disorder/dyslexia will have another disorder as well"; some meet criteria for Developmental Language Disorder (one cited study: "some 40% had significant language impairments"); dyslexia is "often comorbid with attentional and motor coordination problems"; maths disorder is comorbid "in between 30% and 70% of the cases". — [Snowling, Hulme & Nation 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7455053/)
- Reading and maths disability comorbidity in grades 2–4 was "approximately 25%"; ADHD is a frequent comorbid externalising disorder. — [Peterson & Pennington 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465717/)

**Speech perception [WEAK/MIXED]**
- Ziegler et al. (2009, *Developmental Science*): dyslexic children showed "clear speech perception deficits in noise but not in silence", persisting against reading-age controls. — [Ziegler et al. 2009](https://read.qxmd.com/read/19702766/speech-perception-in-noise-deficits-in-dyslexia)
- Not replicated consistently: Messaoud-Galusi, Hazan & Rosen (2011, *JSLHR* 54(6); 62 dyslexic, 51 average readers aged 6–13) found no group difference on identification in noise or two word-recognition-in-noise tests; poor-perceiver rates ran 0–40% by task; "most children with dyslexia do not appear to show a consistent deficit in speech perception." — [Messaoud-Galusi et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3374927)

**"Visual-spatial strengths" [MYTH at group level]**
- Chamberlain, Brunswick, Siev & McManus (2018, *British Journal of Psychology* 109(4), 897–916), meta-analysis of 909 non-dyslexic and 956 dyslexic individuals: mean visuo-spatial difference −0.457 (dyslexic lower), SD ratio 1.107 (dyslexic group more variable). The authors call it the first demonstration that a majority of dyslexic individuals show impoverished, and more variable, visuo-spatial processing. — [Chamberlain et al. 2018](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/23430)
- Conflict to note: a search summary of the pre-proof of the same paper gave "an average effect size across all tasks of .72"; the repository abstract gives −0.457. The direction (dyslexic lower) is the same; the magnitude should be checked in the full text. — [Chamberlain et al. pre-proof](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10052683/1/BJP%20dyslexia%20meta-analysis%20pre-proof.pdf)
- The earlier review by Gilger et al. (2016) found no significant dyslexic–control difference in 72% of visual-spatial comparisons, i.e. equal or worse most of the time (as described in the Chamberlain pre-proof). — [Chamberlain et al. pre-proof](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10052683/1/BJP%20dyslexia%20meta-analysis%20pre-proof.pdf); see also [IDA: Why study nonverbal skills in dyslexia?](https://dyslexiaida.org/why-study-nonverbal-skills-in-dyslexia/)
- Visual theories of dyslexia are largely superseded; "various visual treatments (colored lenses, vision therapy)" either "lack sufficient evidence or have been shown to be ineffective" and "should not be recommended". — [Peterson & Pennington 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465717/)

**Learning styles [MYTH]**
- Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9(3), 105–119): "at present, there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice"; they found "virtually no evidence" for the style-by-method interaction that the meshing hypothesis requires (while noting that many versions were never properly tested). — [Pashler et al. 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)
- Weinstein, Madan & Sumeracki (2018): learning styles "have come to be described as a myth or urban legend within psychology"; dual coding is different: it adds complementary words-plus-pictures for *everyone* rather than tailoring to a preference. — [Weinstein et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/)

### Inferences
- The bottleneck for this audience is print decoding speed/accuracy plus a smaller verbal buffer, not understanding. Spoken narration bypasses the decoding bottleneck, so a narrated video is a sound primary channel. Design effort should go to (a) not forcing reading at the video's pace and (b) protecting verbal working memory: short spoken units, few new terms at once, visuals that hold information on screen so it need not be held in the head.
- Because verbal short-term memory and new-word learning are weak points, new *spoken* technical terms are a specific hazard: phonologically novel words are exactly what this group stores poorly. Introduce few, say them clearly, repeat them, and anchor each to a picture and a short on-screen label.
- A large minority of the audience will also have language (DLD), attention (ADHD) or maths difficulties. Design for the harder case: plain vocabulary, explicit structure, no distractions.
- Do not justify visuals with "dyslexic people are visual thinkers". Justify them with the multimedia principle (section 2). Expect *more* spread in visuo-spatial ability, so complex diagrams need the same signaling and step-by-step build-up as for anyone else.
- The IDA phrase "vary depending on the orthography" and the finding that phonological awareness matters more in less consistent orthographies suggest that in a relatively consistent orthography the visible problem is more often slow reading than inaccurate reading. If that holds for Icelandic, the main on-screen-text risk is *time*: text that disappears before a slow reader finishes.

### Gaps
- No Icelandic-specific data were found on the cognitive profile of dyslexic learners, on Icelandic orthographic consistency, or on video learning; the orthography inference above is unsourced for Icelandic.
- I found no pooled meta-analytic estimate specifically for *processing speed* in dyslexia; the link here rests on the rapid-naming/processing-speed correlation in Peterson & Pennington.
- The full IDA 2025 definition text and explanatory notes could not be fetched (HTTP 403); wording above is from search snippets.
- Claims of superior creativity in dyslexia: one search summary mentioned two meta-analyses finding no evidence, but I did not open a primary source, so it is not listed as a finding.

## 2. Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning: which principles matter most, effect sizes, and tests with dyslexic learners

### Takeaway
The best-supported design levers for a narrated explainer are: pair narration with relevant graphics, cut everything extraneous, synchronise words and pictures, signal what matters, break content into segments, pre-teach key terms, and speak conversationally in a human voice; Mayer's own median effects for these run d ≈ 0.6–1.3, though an independent re-analysis of the same corpus pools to only g = 0.37, so treat the big numbers as upper bounds from short lab lessons. Direct tests with dyslexic learners are few, small and mostly user-paced: in children, audio halves study time and (in one study) improves transfer; in university students results are mixed to reversed, so "narration is always better for dyslexia" is only moderately supported and is strongest for children and for system-paced video.

### Cited Findings

**Theory in one paragraph**
- CTML rests on three assumptions: dual channels (visual/pictorial and auditory/verbal), limited capacity in each channel, and active processing (select, organise, integrate). Design aims: reduce extraneous processing, manage essential processing, foster generative processing. — [Mayer 2023, APA Division 2 chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

**Mayer's median effect sizes (Mayer 2021 meta-analysis of his lab's work, reported in Mayer 2023; transfer tests; "tests" = positive results / total). Mayer treats d > 0.40 as educationally important.** All rows: [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

| Principle | What to do | Tests | Median d | Boundary conditions stated by Mayer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multimedia | Words + graphics rather than words alone | 13 comparisons | 1.35 | Not all graphics are equally effective |
| Coherence | Delete extraneous words, pictures, sounds | 18 of 19 | 0.86 | Strongest for **low working-memory capacity**, **system-paced** lessons, and highly interesting extraneous material |
| Signaling | Highlight essential material and its organisation | 26 of 28 | 0.70 (verbal 0.69, 15/16; visual 0.71, 11/12) | Strongest for low-knowledge learners and complex material; use signals **sparingly** |
| Redundancy | Do not add on-screen captions to narrated graphics | 8 of 12 | **0.10** | Diminished or reversed when slow-paced/learner-controlled, text is short or reworded, second language, or no graphics |
| Spatial contiguity | Put printed words next to the part of the graphic they describe | 9 of 9 | 0.82 | Strongest for low prior knowledge, complex lessons |
| Temporal contiguity | Narration at the same time as the matching graphic | 8 of 8 | 1.31 | Strongest for long, system-paced lessons |
| Segmenting | Break lesson into learner-paced parts | 7 of 7 | 0.67 | Stronger when material is complex and lesson is fast-paced |
| Pre-training | Teach names and characteristics of key concepts first | 10 of 10 | 0.78 | May not help high-prior-knowledge learners, simple material, or slow/learner-paced lessons |
| Modality | Spoken rather than printed words with graphics | 18 of 19 | 1.00 | Print can be better for **technical terms**, second language, learner-paced lessons; speech better when fast-paced and words are familiar |
| Personalization | Conversational rather than formal style ("your lungs") | 13 of 15 | 1.00 | May not apply to experienced learners or long lessons |
| Voice | Appealing human voice rather than machine voice | 6 of 7 | 0.74 | Modern TTS is narrowing the gap (Craig & Schroeder 2017, 2018) |
| Embodiment | On-screen instructor gestures, draws, looks; first-person demonstrations | 16 of 17 | 0.58 | May fail with negative social cues (e.g. machine voice) |
| Image | A static picture of the speaker does not necessarily help | 4 of 7 | 0.19 | Static agent image can distract |
| Generative activity | Prompt learners to summarise, explain, draw, self-test | 37 of 44 | 0.71 | — |

- Embodiment details worth using: students learned better when the instructor *drew the graphics while explaining* than when pointing at pre-drawn graphics (Fiorella & Mayer 2016), and from first-person rather than third-person demonstrations (Fiorella et al. 2017). — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

**Independent checks: effects are real but smaller [STRONG caveat]**
- Cromley & Chen (2025, *Educational Research Review* 49, 100730) meta-analysed Mayer's own multimedia research 1990–2022 (92 articles, 181 studies, 591 effect sizes): pooled **g = 0.37**, with a small decline per publication year. Largest significant effects: removing seductive details, modality, personalization, the multimedia principle, sentence-level coherence, self-explanation; medium: testing, scaffolding, cueing (signaling), embodiment. Text + diagrams gave large, consistent effects; animation, games and simulations were less consistent; VR showed no significant effect. — [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)
- Broader-literature signaling meta-analysis (Alpizar, Adesope & Wong 2020): 29 studies, 44 effect sizes, 2,726 participants, d = 0.38. — [Alpizar et al. 2020 (summary)](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/812517839771533313)
- Segmenting meta-analysis (Rey et al. 2019, *Educational Psychology Review* 31, 389–419): 56 investigations, 88 comparisons; significant small-to-medium effects on retention and transfer, attributed to designer-imposed chunking/structure and to extra processing time (snippet-level). — [Rey et al. 2019 (summary)](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/a-meta-analysis-of-the-segmenting-effect/812814232193073153-10463)
- Modality across the broader literature: median d = 0.65 across 76 studies (Fiorella & Mayer 2022; snippet-level via secondary summary). — [Hendrick, "Which multimedia strategies actually improve learning?"](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually)

**Transient-information limit on narration [MODERATE]**
- Leahy & Sweller (2011, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 25(6), 943–951) and their follow-up (2016, *Instructional Science*): with **long** spoken segments the modality effect reversed (written beat spoken) because speech vanishes and must be held in working memory; shortening the spoken text restored the usual advantage for narration. — [Leahy & Sweller 2011](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-and-the-effects-of-transient-information-on/); [Leahy & Sweller follow-up](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-015-9362-9)

**Studies with dyslexic learners [WEAK/MIXED: small samples, mostly user-paced lessons, Dutch and US]**
- Children, study 1 (Knoop-van Campen, Segers & Verhoeven 2018, *Dyslexia* 24(2), 140–155): 26 dyslexic and 38 typical 11-year-olds; user-paced science lessons, 12 slides; pictures with text, with audio, or with text + audio; retention and transfer tested immediately and after one week. Dyslexic children's study time: text 8.32 min (SD 4.38) vs audio 4.90 min vs text + audio 5.00 min (text vs audio ηp² = .327; text vs text + audio ηp² = .346). Typical readers: no time difference (4.57 / 4.95 / 5.03 min). **No modality or redundancy effect on knowledge gain** in either group; working memory did not explain the effects. Authors: "provide children with dyslexia information in an auditory way". — [Knoop-van Campen et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6084336)
- Children, study 2 (Knoop-van Campen, Segers & Verhoeven 2019, *Research in Developmental Disabilities* 90): 21 dyslexic and 21 typical 5th graders, user-paced. No effects on retention. On **transfer**, dyslexic children showed a modality effect (audio + pictures better); typical children showed a reversed redundancy effect (text + pictures better). Text-only was slowest for both groups. Executive functions (verbal and visual working memory, inhibition, flexibility) related to learning but did not alter the effects. — [Knoop-van Campen et al. 2019](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2019/journal-article-abstracts/s0891422219300666/)
- University students (Knoop-van Campen, Segers & Verhoeven 2020, *Computers & Education* 150, 103858): 42 dyslexic and 44 typical students, eye-tracking, user-paced; text + pictures versus text + audio + pictures. Adding audio to text lengthened study time, increased looking at pictures and text–picture transitions, and produced a **negative redundancy effect on transfer** in both groups, with no effect on retention. Authors: "students with dyslexia should only use audio support when aiming to learn factual knowledge". — [Knoop-van Campen et al. 2020](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/effects-of-audio-support-on-multimedia-learning-processes-and-out/)
- Secondary students, text reading with read-aloud audio (Knoop-van Campen, ter Doest, Verhoeven & Segers 2022, *Annals of Dyslexia*): 21 dyslexic and 22 typical; audio support increased reading time in most tasks, did not change comprehension, and reduced selective reading strategies (snippet-level). — [Knoop-van Campen et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11881-021-00246-w.pdf)
- US college students (Dawson, Zhu, Ritzhaupt et al. 2021, *Annals of Dyslexia* 71(1), 188–210): 148 students with and without dyslexia; images present/absent × narration/on-screen text. Performance was better with images across groups; a **reverse modality effect** was reported, with dyslexic students outperforming peers in on-screen-text conditions (snippet-level). — [Dawson et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-021-00219-z)
- US college students (Wang et al. 2018, *Journal of Special Education Technology*): 73 students with dyslexia; on-screen text gave better recall and recognition than spoken text; the modality principle did not hold (snippet-level). — [Wang et al. 2018](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2075222276)

**Text-to-speech / audio support for reading [MODERATE]**
- Wood, Moxley, Tighe & Wagner (2018, *Journal of Learning Disabilities* 51(1), 73–84): meta-analysis of 22 group-design studies (1995–2014); text-to-speech and read-aloud tools improved reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities, average weighted effect 0.35 (95% CI 0.14–0.56). — [Wood et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494021)
- Keelor et al. (2023): TTS, with or without highlighting, gave significantly better comprehension than silent reading in children with reading and language difficulties (snippet-level; no sample size or effect size retrieved). — [Keelor et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37119436/)

### Inferences
- Ranking for this audience, combining effect size, fit to the dyslexic profile, and the fact that video is system-paced and transient: (1) narration + meaningful graphics; (2) coherence, whose effect Mayer says is strongest for low working-memory learners and system-paced lessons, which describes this audience watching video; (3) temporal contiguity, since a viewer with a weak verbal buffer cannot hold a sentence while waiting for its picture; (4) segmenting and built-in pauses; (5) pre-training of the few key terms, because new spoken words are a known weak spot; (6) signaling, used sparingly; (7) spatial contiguity for any labels; (8) conversational human voice.
- The dyslexia studies do not contradict using narration for video. Almost all were *user-paced* slide lessons where a reader can take as long as needed; the cost of print for dyslexic children showed up as time (8.3 vs 4.9 minutes). In a video the clock is fixed, so that time cost would become missed content. The reverse-modality findings come from university students, a selected group of compensated readers, studying at their own pace.
- The university finding that text + audio hurt transfer is a warning against full-sentence on-screen text duplicating narration, even for dyslexic learners (see section 3).
- Mayer's boundary condition "print can be better for technical terms" plus the transient-information effect together imply: say it in short units, and leave the technical term visibly on screen.
- Treat Mayer's medians as best-case. A realistic expectation per principle in real materials is closer to g ≈ 0.2–0.5, but the principles are cheap to apply and their benefits plausibly accumulate.

### Gaps
- No study was found testing coherence, signaling, segmenting, pre-training, personalization, voice or embodiment specifically in dyslexic samples. Only modality and redundancy have been tested, in roughly six studies.
- No study was found of dyslexic learners with *system-paced video* (as opposed to user-paced slides), which is the exact use case.
- Kim, Wiseheart & Walden (2018, *Journal of Postsecondary Education and Disability* 31(4)) on multimedia design for college students with dyslexia was identified but its findings were not retrieved.
- Exact pooled effect sizes for Rey et al. (2019) segmenting, and the moderator split between learner-paced and system-paced segmenting, were not retrieved.

## 3. On-screen text, captions and typography for dyslexic viewers

### Takeaway
The redundancy principle is weak (median d = 0.10) and does not show that captions harm learning; what the evidence does support is: keep narration as the carrier, avoid full-sentence text that duplicates it during complex visuals, and use short keyword labels placed on the graphic (which improved retention, d = 0.47–0.70, in typical learners). There is no good experimental evidence that same-language captions or karaoke-style word highlighting improve *content learning* for dyslexic viewers; offer captions as a viewer-controlled option rather than burning them in. On typography, special dyslexia fonts do not work; a clean sans-serif, generous size and spacing, and short lines are reasonable, but the British Dyslexia Association guide is consensus guidance, not trial-tested evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Redundancy and captions**
- Mayer's redundancy principle ("People do not learn more deeply when on-screen text is added to narrated graphics") was supported in 8 of 12 tests with a median effect of only d = 0.10; it is "diminished or even reversed" when the lesson is slow-paced or learner-controlled, the text is short or reworded, the words are in a second language, or there are no graphics. Proposed mechanisms: reconciling two verbal streams, and looking at the text instead of the graphic. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Mayer & Johnson (2008, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 100(2), 380–386): adding **short phrases inside the diagram** (two to three key words echoing the narration) to a narrated lesson improved retention (d = 0.47 and 0.70 in two experiments) with no gain or loss on transfer; explanation: short labels guide attention without adding extraneous processing (snippet-level, from abstract). — [Mayer & Johnson 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380)
- Adesope & Nesbit (2012, *Journal of Educational Psychology*): meta-analysis of 48 effect sizes from 32 reports; spoken + written redundancy helped mainly low-prior-knowledge learners with system-paced materials **when no images were shown concurrently**; results were heterogeneous (snippet-level). — [Adesope & Nesbit 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147)
- Dyslexia-specific: in university students with and without dyslexia, text + audio + pictures gave *worse transfer* than text + pictures; in dyslexic children, text + audio was as time-efficient as audio alone and no worse for learning. — [Knoop-van Campen et al. 2020](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/effects-of-audio-support-on-multimedia-learning-processes-and-out/); [Knoop-van Campen et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6084336)
- Spatial contiguity: words placed next to the relevant part of the graphic beat the same words as a caption at the bottom of the screen (Moreno & Mayer 1999); median d = 0.82 over 9 of 9 tests. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

**"Captions benefit everyone" [WEAK/MIXED for this audience]**
- Gernsbacher (2015, *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 2(1), 195–202) states that "more than 100 empirical studies document that captioning a video improves comprehension of, attention to, and memory for the video", with particular benefit for non-native viewers, people learning to read, and D/deaf or hard-of-hearing viewers. — [Gernsbacher 2015](https://successforkidswithhearingloss.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Video-Captions-Benefit-Everyone_2015.pdf)
- Critique (Andrew Watson, Learning and the Brain blog, 22 June 2025): the cited studies mostly concern foreign-language captions, non-neurotypical learners, college students, or self-report; he found no objective-measure studies of neurotypical K–12 students with same-language captions, and warns of split attention on complex material. One study he cites (Linebarger, Piotrowski & Greenwood 2010) found captions helped at-risk 2nd–3rd graders *recognise words* while not-at-risk children recognised fewer words with captions on. — [Watson 2025](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/video-captions-benefit-everyone-an-investigation/)
- A comparison of learning from text, video, or subtitled video (*Computers & Education*, 2021) found equivalent immediate comprehension but a "disadvantage of subtitled videos for deep learning outcomes" (snippet-level). — [Learning from text, video, or subtitles](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131520302323)
- Survey data only (not experiments): 61% of students with learning disabilities said captions were helpful (Oregon State survey, 2016). — [Campus Technology report](https://campustechnology.com/Articles/2016/11/02/Report-Video-Captions-Benefit-Virtually-All-Students.aspx)
- Subtitle speed matters even for typical adult readers: in an eye-tracking study at 12, 20 and 28 characters per second, faster subtitles reduced the chance to read all words, reread, or finish the subtitle. — [Kruger et al., "Why subtitle speed matters"](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/why-subtitle-speed-matters-evidence-from-word-skipping-and-rereading/A7DB0CBF5910353143738CF9C1721317)

**Karaoke-style / synchronised highlighting [WEAK]**
- Keelor et al. (2023): text-to-speech improved comprehension over silent reading for children with reading and language difficulties, but **highlighting made no measurable difference**: TTS with and without highlighting performed alike. — [Keelor et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37119436/)
- A pilot study with dyslexic children reported that reading along *felt easier* with audio synchronised to highlighting (a band over the whole sentence was liked best); it measured ease, not comprehension. — [Pilot study on highlighting text, iJET 2018](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jet/article/download/8736/5161/29419)
- Vendor and blog claims of large gains (for example "34% better comprehension") could not be traced to a verifiable study and should not be used. — [example of such a claim](https://castreader.ai/blog/tts-for-dyslexia)

**Dyslexia fonts [MYTH: they do not help]**
- Wery & Diliberto (2017, *Annals of Dyslexia* 67(2), 114–127): 12 dyslexic students in grades 3–6, alternating-treatment design; OpenDyslexic versus Arial and Times New Roman on letter naming, word reading and nonsense-word reading. "No evidence" of improved rate or accuracy; improvement-rate differences were *negative* for OpenDyslexic on every task (fluency about −50% to −89%; accuracy about −54% to −76%); no student preferred it. — [Wery & Diliberto 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates (2013), as reported by Wery & Diliberto: OpenDyslexic did not significantly reduce reading time or fixations in Spanish readers with dyslexia. — [Wery & Diliberto 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233)
- Kuster et al. (2018, *Annals of Dyslexia* 68, 25–42), "Dyslexie font does not benefit reading in children with or without dyslexia"; an experiment with 170 dyslexic children found no letterform effect; given a choice, readers tended to prefer Arial (snippet-level). — [Kuster et al. as described in Galliussi et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700); [OpenDyslexic, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenDyslexic)
- Marinus et al. (2016, *Dyslexia* 22(3), 233–244): low-progress readers read about 7% more words per minute in Dyslexie than in standard Arial, but the difference vanished when Arial was given the same letter and word spacing; the benefit is spacing, not letter shape. — [Marinus et al. 2016](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/a-special-font-for-people-with-dyslexia-does-it-work-and-if-so-wh/)
- Galliussi et al. (2020, *Annals of Dyslexia* 70, 141–152): 128 Italian children (64 dyslexic, mean age 12.4); letterform × letter spacing × word spacing. Letterform had no effect on speed or accuracy; spacing effects were small (never above 0.20 syllables/second) and inconsistent; the one robust result was that **increasing letter spacing without also increasing word spacing slowed reading** in both groups. — [Galliussi et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700)
- Spacing evidence is contested: Zorzi et al. (2012) found extra-large letter spacing helped dyslexic children, but the sentences were not length-matched; Hakvoort et al. (2017) found fewer errors but no speed gain under matched conditions. — [Galliussi et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700)

**British Dyslexia Association style guide [PRACTITIONER consensus, not trial evidence]**
(From an earlier BDA "Dyslexia Style Guide" edition hosted by Wigan Council; the current 2023 edition could not be fetched.) — all bullets: [BDA Dyslexia Style Guide (earlier edition)](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)
- Fonts: "Use a plain, evenly spaced sans serif font such as Arial and Comic Sans. Alternatives include Verdana, Tahoma, Century Gothic, Trebuchet."
- Size: "Font size should be 12-14 point. Some dyslexic readers may request a larger font." (This is for print and documents.)
- Colour: "Avoid white backgrounds for paper, computer and visual aids. White can appear too dazzling. Use cream or a soft pastel colour." "Use dark coloured text on a light (not white) background." "Avoid green and red/pink as these are difficult for colour-blind individuals."
- Emphasis: "Avoid underlining and italics ... Use bold instead." "AVOID TEXT IN BLOCK CAPITALS: this is much harder to read." Headings: larger, bold, lower case. Boxes and borders may be used for emphasis.
- Layout: left-justified with ragged right edge; avoid narrow columns; "Lines should not be too long: 60 to 70 characters"; "Line spacing of 1.5 is preferable"; avoid dense paragraphs; prefer bullet points and numbering to continuous prose.
- Writing style: "Use short, simple sentences in a direct style"; active voice; avoid double negatives; be concise; avoid abbreviations or give a glossary; flow charts are "ideal for explaining procedures"; aim for Flesch Reading Ease of about 70–80 "by using short sentences, not by dumbing down vocabulary".
- Motion: "Moving text creates problems for people with visual difficulties."
- The guide's stated rationale is "the visual stress experienced by some dyslexic people". Independent reviews say visual treatments such as coloured lenses lack evidence for dyslexia. — [BDA guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf); [Peterson & Pennington 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465717/)
- Dyslexia Scotland's typed-format guidance likewise suggests line spacing of about 1.5 (snippet-level). — [Dyslexia Scotland](https://dyslexiascotland.org.uk/dyslexia-friendly-typed-formats)

### Inferences
- Does the redundancy principle mean captions hurt? No. At d = 0.10 with four of twelve tests not supporting it, and with reversals for short text and slow pacing, it is a reason to avoid *long duplicated text over complex visuals*, not a reason to ban captions. For slow readers the real problem is different: timed text they cannot finish steals attention from both the picture and the narration.
- Practical rule set that fits all the evidence above:
  1. Narration carries the explanation. Nothing essential should exist only as on-screen text.
  2. On-screen words are labels: one to four words, the key term or number, placed on or beside the thing they name, appearing as the narrator says them and staying until the idea is finished.
  3. No full-sentence text that duplicates narration while a diagram or animation is being explained.
  4. Offer captions as an optional toggle (closed captions), not burned in. This serves deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers and sound-off viewing without imposing a reading load on those it hinders.
  5. Never animate, scroll or bounce the text itself.
  6. Any text screen (definition, formula, summary) should be held long enough for a slow reader and be read aloud in full by the narrator.
- Word-by-word karaoke highlighting is a reading-practice device. For subject learning it has no demonstrated benefit, forces eye-tracking of text at the narrator's pace, and pulls gaze from the explanatory graphic. Highlighting a *label or part of the diagram* as it is mentioned is signaling (d ≈ 0.4–0.7) and is the better use of the same idea.
- Typography for the few words that do appear: ordinary sans-serif (no special dyslexia font), large, bold for emphasis, sentence case rather than capitals, no italics or underline, dark on a light non-glaring or otherwise high-contrast background, generous word spacing whenever letter spacing is widened. The off-white background advice is low-cost and harmless but rests on preference and the unproven "visual stress" account.
- The 12–14 pt figure is for print. For video the equivalent rule is "readable at a glance on a phone", which means few words set large; there is no evidence-based point size for video.

### Gaps
- I found **no controlled experiment** on same-language captions or subtitles and content learning in dyslexic viewers (children or adults). This is a real hole in the literature as far as this search reached, not just in these notes.
- No study was found on karaoke-style highlighted captions *in video* for dyslexic learners; the evidence is from text-to-speech reading tools.
- The current BDA Dyslexia Friendly Style Guide (2023) could not be retrieved (HTTP 403/503). My unverified recollection is that it adds inter-letter spacing of about 35% of average letter width, inter-word spacing of at least 3.5 times the letter spacing, line spacing of 150%, and names further fonts such as Calibri and Open Sans; confirm against the BDA site before citing.
- No evidence-based guidance was found on Icelandic-specific typography (for example legibility of þ, ð, æ, ö and accented vowels in particular typefaces).
- Full statistics for Mayer & Johnson (2008) and Adesope & Nesbit (2012) were not read; figures above are from abstracts.

## 4. Narration pacing: speech rate, pauses, sentence length and vocabulary load

### Takeaway
There is no experimental evidence defining an optimal narration rate for dyslexic learners; the defensible guidance comes from mechanism: speech is transient and this audience has a smaller verbal buffer, so use short spoken units, pause at idea boundaries, limit and pre-teach new terms, and give the viewer control (pause, replay, speed). Ordinary conversational instructional speech sits around 150–160 words per minute in English; aiming at or somewhat below that with real pauses is an inference, not a tested rule, and no Icelandic-specific rate data were found.

### Cited Findings
- Transient-information effect: long spoken segments overload working memory and can make narration *worse* than text; short spoken segments restore the advantage of narration (Leahy & Sweller 2011; follow-up 2016). — [Leahy & Sweller 2011](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-and-the-effects-of-transient-information-on/); [follow-up](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-015-9362-9)
- Mayer's modality boundary conditions: spoken words work best when "presented in segments" small enough to be held in working memory and when the words are familiar; printed words can be better when the material contains technical terms. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Segmenting: breaking a narrated animation into 16 segments of about 10 seconds, each advanced by the learner, improved transfer (Mayer & Chandler 2001); median d = 0.67 across 7 of 7 tests; stronger for complex, fast-paced material. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Pre-training on names and characteristics of key components before the main explanation: median d = 0.78 across 10 of 10 tests. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Verbal signaling includes an outline, headings, pointer words ("first, second, third") and **vocal stress on key words**; verbal signaling median d = 0.69 (15 of 16 tests). — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Dyslexic children had much weaker verbal working memory than classmates (d = 1.24). — [Knoop-van Campen et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6084336)
- Dyslexia involves difficulty learning new spoken words and poor verbal short-term memory. — [Snowling, Hulme & Nation 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7455053/)
- Observed speaking rates in real instructional video: across edX videos (6.9 million viewing sessions, four courses) speaking rate ranged 48–254 wpm, mean 156 wpm (SD 31). Engagement was higher for faster speakers (up to 2×), and for 6–12-minute videos dipped in the 145–165 wpm band. This measured **engagement (watch time) in adult MOOC learners, not learning**, and the authors call speaking rate "merely a surface feature" correlated with instructor enthusiasm. — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- Same study: median engagement time was at most 6 minutes regardless of video length; recommendation to plan lessons into chunks shorter than 6 minutes; pre-production planning had the largest impact on engagement. — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- Typical conversational speech is about 150 wpm, and comprehension of speeded speech starts to fall off near 275 wpm in typical adults. — ["The Effect of Video Playback Speed on Learning and Mind-Wandering in Younger and Older Adults"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10330257/)
- Industry voice-over guides suggest roughly 130–150 wpm for instructional narration; these are trade conventions, not research findings. **[PRACTITIONER]** — [Handysides voice-over guide](https://kimhandysidesvoiceover.com/2022/08/16/timing-in-elearning-videos/)
- BDA writing-style advice, which transfers directly to spoken scripts: short, simple sentences in a direct style; active voice; no double negatives; avoid abbreviations and jargon or explain them; achieve readability "by using short sentences, not by dumbing down vocabulary". **[PRACTITIONER]** — [BDA Dyslexia Style Guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)
- Speech-in-noise difficulty in dyslexia was reported by Ziegler et al. (2009) but not consistently replicated (Messaoud-Galusi et al. 2011). — [Ziegler et al. 2009](https://read.qxmd.com/read/19702766/speech-perception-in-noise-deficits-in-dyslexia); [Messaoud-Galusi et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3374927)
- Background music and sound are extraneous material under the coherence principle (median d = 0.86 for removing extraneous material), with the largest penalty for low working-memory learners in system-paced lessons. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

### Inferences
- Pauses matter more than raw words per minute. A viewer with a small verbal buffer needs a beat after each idea to integrate it with the picture; system-paced pauses of one to two seconds at idea boundaries are the video equivalent of learner-paced segmenting. Rey et al.'s attribution of segmenting benefits partly to extra processing time supports this, though the exact pause length is my estimate.
- One idea per sentence; put the subject and verb early; avoid nested clauses that force the listener to hold the opening while waiting for the end. Sentences of roughly 8–15 words are a sensible working target (my heuristic; no study gives a number).
- Vocabulary load: ration new technical terms to perhaps two or three per short video; introduce each with a picture and an on-screen label, say it slowly the first time, then reuse the *same* word every time (do not vary synonyms for style, since each new word form costs phonological learning).
- Icelandic consideration (inference): long compound words and inflected forms mean a technical term may be heard in several case forms. Showing the base form as a stable on-screen label while the narrator uses it in running speech should help, and words per minute is a poor cross-language metric because Icelandic words are longer on average; judge pace by ideas per minute and by pauses.
- Keep the audio clean: no music bed under explanation, no sound effects competing with speech. The dyslexia-specific noise evidence is mixed, but coherence evidence alone justifies it.
- The edX result (faster speakers are more engaging) should not be read as "speak fast": it is about enthusiasm in adult volunteers and measures watch time, not understanding. The transferable part is energy and chunks under 6 minutes.
- Platform speed controls and easy replay are a cheap way to let each viewer set the pace; mention them.

### Gaps
- No study was found that manipulates narration rate, pause length or sentence length and measures learning in dyslexic learners. No dyslexia-specific words-per-minute recommendation exists in the sources reached.
- No Icelandic speech-rate norms or Icelandic instructional-narration studies were found.
- A 2026 preprint claimed a meta-analysis shows simple material stays comprehensible up to about 1.5× playback; I did not verify it and have not used it.
- Evidence on time-compressed or slowed speech specifically in dyslexia was not found in this search.

## 5. Over-explanation versus under-explanation

### Takeaway
Both failure modes are documented: explanations and supports that help novices become dead weight for learners who already know the material (expertise reversal, redundancy), while experts systematically under-explain because they cannot see what novices lack (curse of knowledge, expert blind spot). The evidence-based way through is to cut everything that is not needed for the learning goal, then spend the saved time on the one or two genuinely hard steps: the prerequisite most viewers lack and the misconception most viewers hold. Meta-analysis suggests that supporting novices pays off more than withholding support from experts costs, so when unsure about a struggling-reader school audience, err toward explaining the hard step fully while deleting the obvious.

### Cited Findings

**Over-explaining and its costs**
- Expertise reversal effect: instructional techniques "that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners" (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, *Educational Psychologist* 38(1)); the mechanism is that guidance redundant with what the learner already knows must still be processed. — [Kalyuga et al., The expertise reversal effect](https://ro.uow.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/The_expertise_reversal_effect/27724950/1); [Kalyuga 2007 review](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
- Meta-analysis (Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod 2025, *Learning and Instruction* 98, 102142): 176 effect sizes, 60 experiments, 5,924 participants; the effect is robust across contexts, strongest in higher education, weaker in language and humanities; **giving novices assistance has a stronger effect than withholding assistance from experts** (snippet-level, via secondary summary). — [Watson, "One size doesn't fit all" (summary of Tetzlaff et al. 2025)](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/)
- Mayer's boundary conditions say the same thing principle by principle: pre-training "may not apply to high prior knowledge learners" or simple material; signaling helps most for low-knowledge learners and complex material and should be used sparingly; personalization may not help experienced learners. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Coherence: deleting extraneous material improved transfer in 18 of 19 tests, median d = 0.86. Examples: removing interesting-but-irrelevant sentences about viruses (Mayer et al. 2008); removing dramatic lightning-strike clips from a lightning lesson (Mayer, Heiser & Lonn 2001). — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Seductive details meta-analysis (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32(3), 707–734): 58 studies, more than 7,500 students; interesting-but-irrelevant content harms learning, reported as g ≈ −0.33 by a secondary source (not verified in the paper); press coverage says harm was greater when the detail sat next to relevant diagrams and in natural-science and social-studies content. A later conference analysis reported a smaller effect (g = −0.16; 177 effects, 50 studies). — [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 (record)](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842); [press summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200319103223.htm); [2026 AERA abstract](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera26/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/2273554/)
- In the independent meta-analysis of Mayer's corpus, removing seductive details was among the largest effects. — [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)
- Fun examples do not necessarily harm learning of the core concept (Baldassari & Kelley 2012), but irrelevant memorable content can (Harp & Mayer 1998). — [Weinstein et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/)

**Under-explaining and its causes**
- Curse of knowledge: the term comes from Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (*Journal of Political Economy*); Hinds (1999, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*), "The curse of expertise", examined how poorly experts predict novice performance and tested debiasing methods. — [Curse of knowledge (overview)](https://en.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge)
- Tullis & Feder (2022, *Memory & Cognition*): studying material made people *worse* at estimating what novices know, and telling them to discount their own knowledge did not fix it. — [Tullis & Feder 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13421-022-01382-3.pdf)
- Leaving misconceptions unaddressed: Muller, Bewes, Sharma & Reimann (2008, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 24(2), 144–155) compared four physics videos (Exposition, Extended Exposition, Refutation, Dialogue). Students learned significantly more from the two that stated and refuted common misconceptions than from the clear expository ones; simply adding more exposition did not help (snippet-level). — [Summary of Muller et al. 2008](https://meadcj.github.io/posts/2023-11-13-saying-the-wrong-thing.html); [Muller & Sharma, Tackling misconceptions with multimedia](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IISME/article/view/6345)
- Refutation texts (state the misconception, say it is wrong, explain why): meta-analysis of 44 comparisons, n = 3,869, g = 0.41 (Schroeder & Kucera 2022, *Educational Psychology Review*); a pre-registered 2025 meta-analysis (71 articles, 294 effect sizes) again found a consistent advantage; Zengilowski et al. (2021) voiced scepticism about earlier claims. **[MODERATE]** — [Schroeder & Kucera 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z); [Danielson et al. 2025](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-refutation-text-in-confronting-scientific-mi/)

**Tools for spending time on the hard step**
- Pre-training (teach the names and characteristics of key parts first): median d = 0.78. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Worked examples in mathematics: meta-analysis, g = 0.48 (Barbieri et al. 2023, *Educational Psychology Review*), similar for initial learning and practice; adding self-explanation prompts unexpectedly reduced the effect relative to plain worked examples. — [Barbieri et al. 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1.pdf)
- Concrete first, then abstract ("concreteness fading": concrete instance, then a pared-down picture, then symbols): supported in a systematic review (Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone 2014) and a classroom study (Fyfe, McNeil & Borjas 2015), though a 2020 review questioned how general the sequence is. **[MODERATE, mainly maths and science]** — [Fyfe et al. 2014](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-014-9249-3); [2020 review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09581-7)
- Multiple examples with different surface details transfer better than one example (Gick & Holyoak 1983); novices latch onto surface features (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser 1981); explicitly stating the link between the example and the abstract idea aids transfer (Berry 1983); when an example and the stated rule conflict, learners follow the example (LeFevre & Dixon 1986). — [Weinstein et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/)
- Planning matters most: in the edX data, pre-production planning had the largest impact on engagement, and the recommendation was to segment into chunks under 6 minutes. — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)

**Scripting heuristics from communicators [PRACTITIONER: no learning-outcome trials found]**
- "But / therefore" rule (Trey Parker and Matt Stone, NYU class talk, about 2011): if the beats of a script are joined by "and then", it is flat; each beat should connect to the next with "therefore" or "but", which supplies causation. — [Perell, The But & Therefore Rule](https://perell.com/note/but-therefore-rule/)
- Randy Olson (scientist turned film-maker) adapted this into the "And, But, Therefore" (ABT) template for science communication: set-up, problem, consequence. — [Business of Story on ABT](https://businessofstory.com/cartmans-storytelling-secret-that-every-cmo-should-steal/)

### Inferences
- A workable pre-scripting procedure, assembled from the findings above:
  1. Write the single thing the viewer should be able to explain or do afterwards. One video, one such goal (inference from segmenting, the under-6-minute engagement finding and the transient-information effect; "one idea per video" has no direct trial).
  2. List the prerequisites that goal depends on. Mark each as "the audience surely has it", "probably has it" or "probably lacks it". The last group is where time goes; the first group gets no airtime at all.
  3. Find the standard misconception for the topic (curriculum guides, teacher knowledge, misconception literature). State it, say why it is tempting, and show why it fails. This is one of the few moves with direct video evidence (Muller) and meta-analytic support (g ≈ 0.4).
  4. Because of the curse of knowledge, do not trust your own sense of what is obvious: test the script on a real novice from the target group and note where they stall; that spot is the hard step.
  5. Delete every sentence, fact, joke and visual that does not serve the goal. If an aside is interesting but irrelevant, it is a seductive detail; cut it or move it to a separate video.
- What "over-explaining" means in practice: restating what the picture already shows, defining everyday words, repeating a point in three phrasings, recaps of recaps, long introductions. What "under-explaining" means: skipping a step that feels automatic to the author, using a term before it is introduced, giving the rule without an example, leaving the misconception standing.
- Dyslexia does not mean low prior knowledge. These viewers may know as much as classmates from listening and discussion, or less because they have read less (the IDA definition names reduced reading experience and knowledge growth as secondary consequences). So keep the *language* simple, but do not automatically pitch the *ideas* lower. Padding out easy steps is the form of over-explanation most likely to feel patronising.
- Since viewers differ, layered design beats one fixed level: an optional short "what you need to know first" segment (pre-training) that knowledgeable viewers can skip, chapter markers, and one main explanation aimed at the genuinely hard step. That respects expertise reversal without knowing each viewer.
- "But / therefore" is consistent with learning science in one respect: it forces causal links between steps, which is what transfer tests reward, and it stops scripts becoming lists of facts. It remains a craft heuristic.
- A recurring structure consistent with the evidence: hook question or misconception; concrete example; the hard step, worked slowly with the picture built up piece by piece; the general rule stated plainly; a second, different-looking example; one-sentence summary.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly tests "one idea per video" or the "but / therefore" structure against learning outcomes.
- Nathan & Petrosino's (2003) "expert blind spot" paper was searched for but not retrieved; only Hinds (1999) and Tullis & Feder (2022) are sourced here, the former via a secondary overview.
- No evidence was found on expertise reversal or coherence effects specifically in dyslexic learners.
- I found no validated, general procedure for identifying "the hard step" in a topic; the procedure above is an inference from prerequisite and misconception research.
- The g = −0.33 figure for Sundararajan & Adesope (2020) and the Tetzlaff et al. (2025) details come from secondary summaries and should be checked in the papers.

## 6. Dual coding and concreteness: concrete examples, analogies, and meaningful versus decorative pictures

### Takeaway
Adding relevant pictures to words is one of the largest and most consistent effects in the field, and concrete examples make abstract ideas easier to understand and remember, but the benefit depends on the picture or example *doing explanatory work*: showing the parts, structure, steps or cause of the thing being explained. Decorative pictures are at best neutral for learning and can dilute the effect of the explanatory ones, and overly rich, interesting or surface-heavy examples can distract or block generalisation.

### Cited Findings
- Multimedia principle: in 13 experimental comparisons, learning from words + graphics beat words alone on transfer, median d = 1.35 (for example narrated animation of a tyre pump versus narration alone). "However, not all graphics are equally effective." — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Independent re-analysis: the multimedia principle was among the largest effects, and text + diagrams gave large, consistent effects across factual, inferential and transfer outcomes; animations, games and simulations were less consistent. — [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)
- With images present, college students with and without dyslexia performed better across conditions. — [Dawson et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-021-00219-z)
- Dual coding theory (Paivio): verbal and pictorial representations of the same content give two retrieval routes; "pictures are also more memorable than words" (Paivio & Csapo 1969, 1973); illustrations help most for multi-part concepts and learners with low prior knowledge (Mayer & Gallini 1990; Eitel & Scheiter 2015). — [Weinstein et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/)
- Caveat: "each representation also increases cognitive load and can lead to over-saturation" (Mayer & Moreno 2003); students given a visually rich example "performed more poorly than students who were not given any visual example" (McNeil et al. 2009). — [Weinstein et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/)
- Picture functions (Carney & Levin 2002, "Pictorial illustrations still improve students' learning from text", *Educational Psychology Review* 14(1), 5–26): representational, organisational, interpretational, transformational and decorational; decorative pictures are the type associated with neutral or negative effects (snippet-level via secondary source). — [Using visual and graphic elements (EdTech Books)](https://edtechbooks.org/id/using_visual_and_graphic_elements)
- Experiment with 7th–8th graders (Lenzner, Schnotz & Müller 2013, *Instructional Science*): decorative pictures were "altogether neither harmful nor beneficial for learning", improved mood and calmness, but **moderated (weakened) the benefit of instructional pictures** (snippet-level). — [Lenzner et al. 2013](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-012-9256-z)
- Meta-analysis of pictures in test items (62 studies): decorative pictures had no meaningful effect on performance, while representational and organisational pictures tended to raise it (snippet-level). — [Meta-analysis, Educational Psychology Review 2024](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-024-09883-0)
- Coherence and seductive visuals: adding exciting but irrelevant video clips (lightning strikes) to a lesson on how lightning forms reduced transfer. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Concrete examples: concrete material is recognised and recalled better than abstract; concrete examples "can make the ideas easier to understand and remember"; several examples with different surface features beat a single example for transfer (Gick & Holyoak 1983); verbalising the link from example to principle helps (Berry 1983). — [Weinstein et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/)
- Limits of concreteness: irrelevant perceptual richness should be avoided (Kaminski & Sloutsky 2013); Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler (2008) argued abstract examples can transfer better than concrete ones (disputed by Reed 2008); examples that are too concrete can hinder generalisation. **[MIXED]** — [Weinstein et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780548/)
- Concreteness fading as the reconciliation: start concrete, strip detail step by step, end with the abstract form. — [Fyfe et al. 2014](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-014-9249-3)
- Supporting mechanics: narration simultaneous with the matching graphic (temporal contiguity, d = 1.31); labels adjacent to parts (spatial contiguity, d = 0.82); arrows, spotlighting and pointing (visual signaling, d = 0.71); drawing the diagram while explaining beats pointing at a finished one (Fiorella & Mayer 2016); a static image of the presenter adds little (image principle, d = 0.19). — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- BDA guidance aligns: "Flow charts are ideal for explaining procedures"; "Pictograms and graphics help to locate information." **[PRACTITIONER]** — [BDA Dyslexia Style Guide](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)

### Inferences
- A test for every visual: "Could a viewer answer a question about the content better because of this picture?" If yes, it is explanatory (it shows what the thing looks like, how parts relate, what happens in which order, or why). If it only sets a mood, it is decoration; drop it, or keep it off-screen during explanation.
- For dyslexic viewers visuals do a second job: they are an external memory that holds the parts and relations in view while the narration moves on, relieving the weak verbal buffer. This follows from the verbal working memory deficit plus temporal contiguity. Build diagrams progressively (add one element as it is named) and keep earlier elements visible instead of cutting to a fresh screen for each sentence.
- Stock footage and b-roll that merely match the topic (a generic lab, a crowd, a spinning globe) are decorative by this definition. Typical "talking over stock clips" explainer style is therefore weaker than a single diagram built up step by step.
- Pick examples that are concrete but plain: familiar from the viewer's everyday life, with as little irrelevant colour as possible; then give a second example that looks different on the surface, and say explicitly what the two share. That last sentence is where the abstract rule is taught.
- Dual coding does not mean "show a picture of every noun". It means the picture carries the *structure* of the idea. An icon next to a word adds little; a diagram of how the parts interact adds a lot.
- This is where the "visual learner" myth and the real evidence meet: the recommendation (use explanatory visuals) is right, the reason (dyslexic people are visual thinkers) is wrong. The distinction matters because the myth licenses decorative imagery and wordless complexity, while the evidence demands explanatory, signaled, narrated visuals.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a meta-analysis on instructional **analogies** specifically; analogical transfer is covered here only through Gick & Holyoak (1983) as cited by Weinstein et al. Treat "analogies help" as plausible and classic but not quantified in these notes.
- Effect sizes for each of Carney & Levin's five picture functions were not retrieved (the classic Levin, Anglin & Carney 1987 meta-analysis was not opened).
- No study was found comparing decorative with explanatory visuals specifically in dyslexic learners.
- Evidence on animation versus static diagrams was reached only through Cromley & Chen's summary (animation effects less consistent than text + diagrams); a dedicated animation meta-analysis was not consulted.
