# Memory exercises and playful engagement in short narrated explainer videos for learners with dyslexia

Notes compiled November 2026. Effect sizes are Cohen's d or Hedges' g as reported by the source. "Verified" means I read the primary paper or its abstract in this session; "secondary" means the number comes from a review, press release or search summary and should be checked before it is quoted as exact. The "Inferences" blocks are my design translations, not findings.

## 1. Retrieval practice and questions built into the video (testing effect, interpolated questions, covert vs overt, feedback)

### Takeaway
Stopping a video every few minutes to make the viewer pull the content back out of memory is the best-supported way to make it stick (roughly g = 0.5-0.6 across meta-analyses, and it also reduces mind wandering and anxiety), but with children it only works when the question is cued so that most viewers get it right, and the correct answer should be given straight after. Answering "in your head" can work for simple facts but is unreliable unless the viewer is pushed to actually answer; saying the answer aloud is the safer instruction and is the response mode that specifically suits learners with dyslexia.

### Cited Findings

**Size and robustness of the testing effect**
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Exp. 1, N = 120 undergraduates aged 18-24, prose passages, free recall with no feedback: after 5 minutes restudy beat testing (81% vs 75%), but after 2 days testing won (68% vs 54%, d = 0.95) and after 1 week testing won (56% vs 42%, d = 0.83). Verified. — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006, Psychological Science](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- Same paper, Exp. 2, N = 180: one study period plus three recall tests (STTT) gave 61% recall after 1 week versus 40% for four study periods (SSSS); proportional forgetting over the week was 14% vs 52%. The repeated-study group was the most confident it would remember, and performed worst. Verified. — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- The authors' own boundary condition: tests "will produce strong effects when they occur relatively soon after learning and permit relatively high levels of performance" (initial recall was about 70%). — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- Three meta-analyses (Phelps 2012; Rowland 2014; Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017) put the average testing effect at g/d = 0.50-0.61; Adesope et al. estimated g = 0.67 for classroom settings. Secondary (as summarized by Greving & Richter). — [Greving & Richter 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021), 222 classroom studies, 48,478 students: quizzing raises attainment by g = 0.499; moderators include corrective feedback, number of test repetitions and format match; the effect is reported in primary, secondary and post-secondary education. — [Yang et al. 2021, Psychological Bulletin (record)](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated ten study techniques; only practice testing and distributed practice received "high utility". Summarization, rereading, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text were rated "low". — [ScienceDaily summary of Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm); original [DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)

**Question format and feedback**
- Format evidence conflicts: Adesope et al. found multiple-choice practice produced stronger effects than short-answer; Rowland (2014) found the opposite; a university field study found a testing effect for short-answer but none for multiple-choice questions. — [Greving & Richter 2018](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- Feedback (mostly simply showing the correct answer) is identified as a reliable moderator that increases the effect; Rowland found no testing effect in lab studies without corrective feedback when initial retrieval success was 50% or lower. — [Greving & Richter 2018](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)

**Interpolated questions in video lectures**
- Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013), Harvard undergraduates, Exp. 1 n = 32, Exp. 2 n = 48: a 21-minute statistics video was cut into four segments of about 5.5 minutes; after each, the tested group got six questions at 20 seconds each (2 minutes). Tested vs restudy vs untested: mind wandering on probes 19% / 39% / 41%; slides with extra notes 24% / 9% / 7%; correct answers on the final segment 89% / 65% / 70%; cumulative final test (pooled) 90% / 76% / 68%. Tested viewers also reported less anxiety about the final test (median 2 vs 4 and 3.5 on a 7-point scale) and lower mental demand. Verified; the page does not say whether quiz feedback was given. — [Szpunar, Khan & Schacter 2013, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)
- Caveats: one lecture, one topic, adult elite-university sample; Schacter's own commentary notes it is unclear whether benefits persist across multiple lectures. — [Schacter & Szpunar commentary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3898677)
- Jing, Szpunar & Schacter (2016) found mind-wandering rates did not differ between tested and restudy groups, although mind wandering hurt the restudy group more, so "it reduces mind wandering" is not the whole mechanism. Secondary (search summary of the paper). — [Jing, Szpunar & Schacter 2016](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/08/jing2016-1-1.pdf)
- Brame (2016) recommends embedding questions between segments of roughly 5 minutes and giving viewers guiding questions; cites Lawson et al. (2006: eight guiding questions raised later test scores) and Vural (2013: embedded questions improved later assessment). — [Brame 2016, CBE-Life Sciences Education](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380)
- Mayer's meta-analytic summary: prompting generative activity during a multimedia lesson helped in 37 of 44 comparisons, median d = 0.71; self-testing specifically d = 0.57 (76 tests). Verified. — [Mayer 2023, Research-Based Principles for Designing Multimedia Instruction](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

**What interactive players add (H5P / Edpuzzle style)**
- Ploetzner (2024) meta-analysis: videos with enhanced interaction features improved learning by g = 0.52. Secondary (abstract-level). — [Ploetzner 2024, Instructional Science](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09693-5)
- A meta-analysis of active-learning strategies in video (2019-2025 studies) reports g = 0.33 for retention (k = 24) and g = 0.28 for comprehension (k = 33); embedded questions were the most common strategy (46%). Secondary, seen only via a document-hosting copy. — [Active Learning Strategies in Video Learning: a Meta-Analysis](https://www.scribd.com/document/924187808/Active-Learning-Strategies-in-Video-Learning-a-Meta-Analysis)
- Individual studies are mixed: some find no post-test benefit of question-embedded video, and some students describe embedded quizzes as a distraction or as anxiety-provoking. Secondary (search summaries of several primary studies; which study said what was not verified). — [TechTrends 2019 study](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11528-019-00374-6); [Journal of Computing in Higher Education 2020 study](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s12528-020-09263-x)
- Segmenting: learner-paced segments beat continuous presentation in 7 of 7 tests, median d = 0.67, strongest when material is complex and the lesson is otherwise fast. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Rey et al. (2019), 56 investigations, 88 comparisons: small-to-medium segmenting effects on retention and transfer, lower cognitive load, longer learning time, and the effects are also confirmed for system-paced segmentation (pauses built into the file). Secondary (abstract). — [Rey et al. 2019, Educational Psychology Review](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4)

**Covert ("in your head") vs overt retrieval**
- Tauber et al. (2018): for key-term definitions, overt retrieval beat covert retrieval and restudy, pooled d = 0.43; the authors argue single facts are retrieved fairly automatically while multi-part answers need an overt response. — [Tauber et al. 2018 (record)](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/does-covert-retrieval-benefit-learning-of-key-term-definitions/)
- A 2025 review notes only a handful of studies compare overt and covert retrieval directly and results are mixed. — [Educational Psychology Review 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10040-4)
- Sumeracki & Castillo (2022), university classes: overt (written) and covert retrieval with cold-calling both beat no retrieval two days later (about 61.5% vs 36.4%) and did not differ from each other (61.54% vs 61.47%). Abel & Roediger (2018): merely being asked to think of an answer did not help and was worse than restudy. Practical conclusion from the authors: learners are unlikely to retrieve covertly unless something pushes them to. — [The Learning Scientists, Feb 2024](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/2/22)

**Children and weaker learners**
- Karpicke, Blunt & Smith (2016), 88 fourth-graders (mean age 10.0): cued retrieval ("fruit: ba___") beat restudy in three experiments (recall 0.55 vs 0.44, d = 0.48; 0.42 vs 0.28, d = 0.56; recognition 0.77 vs 0.66, d = 0.64), with no feedback. The benefit did not depend on reading comprehension or processing speed. Initial retrieval success was 75-87%. Verified. — [Karpicke, Blunt & Smith 2016, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786565)
- The same group's earlier study with fourth-graders using unguided free recall found no benefit five days later; initial retrieval success was only about 8%. — [Karpicke, Blunt & Smith 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786565)
- Working memory: college students with lower working-memory capacity benefited at least as much, and after two days more, from retrieval with feedback (Agarwal et al.); Fordyce et al. (2025) found the benefit held regardless of working memory; a 2020 study found no association. — [The Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/12/15-1); [Fordyce et al. 2025, JML](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2025/2025_Fordyce_et_al_JML.pdf)
- Dyslexia: Wilschut, Sense & van Rijn (2024), N = 118 adults (57 with a self-reported medical diagnosis of dyslexia, 61 typical; mean age about 30), adaptive retrieval practice of 40 Swahili-English pairs with corrective feedback. Modality x group interaction on the final spoken test (estimate 0.69, z = 3.86, p < .001): learners with dyslexia remembered more after answering by speech than by typing, typical learners the reverse; with typing, typical learners outperformed dyslexic learners, with speech the gap largely disappeared. Verified. Caveats: conference paper, adults, vocabulary only, single session, speech-recognition errors lowered in-session accuracy. — [Wilschut, Sense & van Rijn 2024, CogSci Proceedings](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/57w2x9vr)
- Agarwal's non-systematic scan of 30+ studies with neurodiverse learners (ADHD, developmental language disorder, Down syndrome, dyslexia; samples usually under 50, ages about 4-40) found retrieval practice generally beat restudy; she notes very little research exists for dyslexia specifically. — [retrievalpractice.org, neurodiversity](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/neurodiversity)
- Children with developmental language disorder (often comorbid with dyslexia) retained new words better after repeated retrieval than after more study (Leonard et al. 2019, 2020). — [The Learning Scientists, Apr 2023](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2023/4/20?format=amp)

### Inferences
- A workable in-video pattern, given a plain video file: explain for 60-120 seconds, then a spoken question with the question restated visually as a picture (not text), a visible "your turn" pause, then the answer shown and spoken. Szpunar's segments were about 5.5 minutes for adults; for children with attention and working-memory limits, shorter segments are a reasonable extrapolation but untested.
- Questions should be cued and winnable. Karpicke's success came with 75-87% initial success; the failure came with 8%. For this audience: give the category or first sound, offer two or three pictures to choose from, or ask for one word. Avoid "tell me everything you remember" as the only format.
- Always give the answer after the pause. Because a plain video cannot know what the viewer said, phrase feedback so it works for both outcomes ("If you said the heart, yes. If you said the lungs, good guess, lots of people do, and here is the difference").
- Use an explicit overt instruction ("say it out loud", "point to it on the screen", "hold up fingers") rather than "think about it". This addresses the finding that covert retrieval often simply does not happen, and matches the speech advantage found for dyslexic learners.
- Mix formats: picture-choice (recognition, high success, good early in an episode) and one-word or one-sentence spoken recall (harder, better later). The format literature conflicts, so neither should be dropped.
- Pause length is not settled by the literature. Reference points: 20 seconds per written question for adults (Szpunar); Blue's Clues set pause lengths by formative testing with children. A defensible starting point is about 3-5 seconds for a picture choice and 6-10 seconds for a spoken answer, with a visible timer and a reminder that the viewer can pause, then tune by watching real learners.
- An interactive player adds three things a file cannot: the video really waits, feedback depends on the actual answer, and picture answers can be tapped. Worth offering as an optional layer.

### Gaps
- I could not open the full text of Adesope et al. (2017). My unverified recollection is g = 0.61 overall, 0.51 against restudy, larger effects for multiple-choice (about 0.70) than short-answer (about 0.48), little difference between feedback and no-feedback, and a strong effect in secondary schools; these must be checked against the paper (DOI 10.3102/0034654316689306) before use.
- No study was found that tests in-video retrieval pauses in a non-interactive video specifically with dyslexic children. The dyslexia evidence is adult vocabulary learning.
- No evidence was found on optimal pause duration for spoken answers in educational video for school-age children.
- No direct comparison of "answer aloud" vs "answer silently" while watching a video was found for children.
- Optimal question frequency is not established; Szpunar tested only one spacing (every 5.5 minutes).

## 2. Exercises that need no reading or writing (prediction, say-aloud, drawing, explaining, enacting)

### Takeaway
Guess-first questions, explaining to someone, imagining, acting out and drawing all have positive evidence (median d about 0.4-0.8 in mostly older-student lab studies), and all can be done by listening, looking and speaking. Prediction questions help only for the thing asked about, are weaker in grade-school children, and need the answer to arrive afterwards; generative tasks need scaffolding or they overload.

### Cited Findings

**Prediction / prequestions**
- St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2024), preregistered meta-analysis, 76 records, about 7,900 participants: prequestions improve learning of the questioned material (g = 0.54, 95% CI 0.42-0.66, k = 97) but not of other material in the lesson (g = 0.04, k = 91). Verified. — [St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Actually guessing matters: g = 0.65 when learners had to produce a guess versus g = 0.22 when they only read the question (p < .001), the strongest moderator in the analysis. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Age: adults g = 0.62 versus grade-school children g = 0.22 (95% CI -0.11 to 0.56; p = .020; k = 20). The authors caution against strong conclusions because of few child studies. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Other moderators: factual prequestions g = 0.58 vs conceptual g = 0.28 (p = .073); multiple-choice 0.51 vs short-answer 0.58 (no difference); audiovisual materials g = 0.66 vs text 0.51 for the specific effect, and a small general benefit only with audiovisual material (g = 0.17 vs -0.005, p = .017); experimenter-paced lessons g = 0.65 vs self-paced 0.42; number of prequestions was not a moderator. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- The benefit depends on the learner noticing the answer later: when learners could not find the answer in the material, performance was at chance (St. Hilaire & Carpenter 2020, as reviewed). The paper links the effect to curiosity and knowledge gaps (Loewenstein 1994) but calls that account under-specified. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Carpenter & Toftness (2017), 85 students, a video in three segments of about 2 minutes, two prequestions before each segment for half the viewers: prequestions improved memory for questioned and (in this study) non-questioned content. Later classroom work found benefits a few days later that had faded after a month. Secondary. — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/heres-simple-way-boost-your-learning-videos-prequestion)
- Pan & Carpenter (2023) review: pretesting helps if there is an opportunity to learn the correct answers afterwards. Secondary (abstract). — [Pan & Carpenter 2023, Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5)

**Generative activities (Fiorella & Mayer)**
- Median effect sizes for prompted generative strategies: summarizing d = 0.50 (30 tests), mapping 0.62 (39), drawing 0.40 (28), imagining 0.65 (22), self-testing 0.57 (76), self-explaining 0.61 (54), teaching 0.77 (19), enacting 0.51 (49). Verified. Boundary: most powerful with training and guidance and when scaffolded so they are not too demanding; most evidence is short-term lab work. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); source review [Fiorella & Mayer 2016](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-015-9348-9)
- Self-explanation meta-analysis (Bisra et al. 2018): 69 effect sizes, g = 0.55. — [Bisra et al. 2018, Educational Psychology Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)
- Drawing: reviews conclude learner-generated drawing beats reading text alone and is generally more effective than writing summaries (Fiorella & Zhang 2018; Cromley et al. 2019). Secondary. — [Ainsworth & Scheiter review](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/index.php/OutputFile/5808285)

**Saying it aloud (production effect)**
- Pritchard, Heron-Delaney, Malone & MacLeod (2020): 7-10-year-olds recognised words they had said aloud better than words read silently, equally for real words and nonwords. — [Pritchard et al. 2020 (record)](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8x35z/the-production-effect-improves-memory-in-7-to-10-year-old-children)
- The effect is reliable for recognition, weaker or absent for free recall in an updated meta-analysis, appears for mouthing as well as speaking, and needs a distinct response per item (saying "yes" each time does nothing). A 2024 study found reading text aloud helped memory but not comprehension. Secondary. — [MacLeod & Bodner 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417691356); [Learning and the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/speaking-writing-and-learning-what-the-production-effect-can-and-cant-do/)

**Ask-pause-respond in children's television**
- Blue's Clues was built around asking the audience questions and pausing; pause lengths were set through formative research so the youngest had time to think and the oldest did not get bored; episodes were tested with children before airing. Secondary. — [Wikipedia, Interactive television (narrative technique)](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactive_television_(narrative_technique)); [APS Observer, Watch and Learn](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/watch-and-learn)
- Anderson et al. (2000): regular viewers showed cognitive gains in a longitudinal comparison, and the habit of answering the screen transferred to another programme. Secondary. — [Anderson et al. 2000, Media Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1207/S1532785XMEP0202_4)
- Crawley et al. (1999): the same episode shown on five consecutive days to 3-5-year-olds kept attention high; repetition is reported to increase comprehension and overt participation. Secondary; a follow-up found experienced viewers interacted more but did not comprehend a new episode better, so the comprehension claim needs checking. The DOI link was built from the journal citation (91(4), 630-637) and not opened. — [Crawley et al. 1999, Journal of Educational Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.91.4.630); [Mental Floss summary](https://www.mentalfloss.com/entertainment/tv/why-blues-clues-repeated-same-episode-five-days)
- Piotrowski (2014), Dora the Explorer with cues removed vs intact: participatory cues helped only children already familiar with the format; unfamiliar children learned less with the cues. — [CcaM summary](https://ccam-ascor.nl/news-archive/371-kids-learning-tv); [Wikipedia, Participatory cues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participatory_cues)
- The fixed "ask, pause, generic praise" format is called pseudo-interaction and is criticised because it cannot respond to what the child said; children learned maths content better when the on-screen character responded contingently. Secondary. — [NSF-hosted study on responsive video characters](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10346101)
- All of this television evidence is from preschoolers, not school-age learners.

### Inferences
- Use one prediction question at the very start of an episode about the single most important fact, make the viewer commit ("Say your guess now. Left picture or right picture?"), and resolve it clearly inside the same episode. Because the benefit is specific, a prediction question is a spotlight for one idea, not a general attention booster.
- Since the child effect is small and uncertain (g = 0.22), treat prediction mainly as a curiosity and engagement device for this audience and rely on retrieval after the content for memory.
- Keep prediction questions factual and concrete; conceptual "why" prequestions showed smaller effects.
- Keep the ask-pause-answer format identical in every episode. Piotrowski's result suggests the format itself has to be learned before it helps, so consistency across a series matters and the first episodes should teach the routine.
- End-of-video generative prompts that need no literacy: "explain it to someone at home, or to your pet" (teaching, d = 0.77), "close your eyes and picture it" (imagining, 0.65), "show it with your hands" (enacting, 0.51), "draw it" (drawing, 0.40). Give a concrete scaffold each time (what to include), since unguided generative tasks are the ones that fail.
- Avoid generic praise after pauses ("Great job!") since it is not contingent and older children will read it as fake; state the answer and the reason instead.

### Gaps
- No studies were found testing prequestions, teach-back or drawing prompts specifically with dyslexic learners.
- I did not locate the Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009) or Kornell pretesting papers directly; they are represented here only through the 2024 meta-analysis that includes them.
- Evidence that viewers actually carry out "explain it to someone" prompts after a video, and at what rate, was not found.
- I found no measured pause length (in seconds) for Blue's Clues or Dora.

## 3. Spacing, recap and cumulative review across a series

### Takeaway
Bringing the same content back for retrieval on later days is as well supported as retrieval itself (spaced vs massed retrieval g = 0.74), and three or more spaced relearning sessions produce large retention gains. A recap that asks the viewer to recall is better supported than a recap that re-tells.

### Cited Findings
- Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021), 29 studies: spaced retrieval practice beats massed retrieval practice, g = 0.74; expanding and uniform schedules do not differ (g = 0.034). Secondary (abstract). — [Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021, Educational Psychology Review (record)](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1340395608147148800)
- Successive relearning (retrieve to a correct answer, then again in later sessions): one-week retention was 68% when items were recalled correctly once in each of three spaced sessions versus 26% when recalled three times in a single session (Rawson et al. 2018). Each extra session added retention. Secondary. — [Rawson & Dunlosky 2022, Current Directions in Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484); [retrievalpractice.org summary](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning)
- About 20% recall one week after a single learning session versus 77% three weeks after a fourth relearning session (different studies, not directly comparable). Secondary. — [Higham et al., Journal of Educational Psychology (preprint)](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/449677/1/Higham_et_al._in_press_JEdPsych.pdf)
- Distributed practice is one of only two techniques rated high utility by Dunlosky et al. (2013), described as effective across ages and ability levels. — [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)
- Restudy-style review produces the illusion of knowing: after repeated study learners were most confident and remembered least a week later, which is the risk of a recap that simply repeats the content. — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- More quizzing of the same content yields larger gains in classroom studies (number of test repetitions is a moderator). — [Yang et al. 2021 (record)](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Repeated viewing of the same episode maintained attention in 3-5-year-olds. — [Crawley et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.91.4.630)
- Rewatching sped-up video just before a test helped adults more than a single earlier viewing (Murphy et al. 2022), i.e. a second exposure close to use has value. — [Murphy et al. 2022, Applied Cognitive Psychology](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)

### Inferences
- Series rule: each episode opens with one or two "last time" recall questions (previous episode) and one "way back" question (three or more episodes earlier). This builds at least three spaced retrievals of each key idea into the series, matching the successive-relearning pattern.
- Since expanding and uniform schedules do not differ, the schedule can be simple and predictable; what matters is that each key idea returns on at least two later days.
- End-of-episode recap should be a recall round ("Three things today. Can you say the first one before I do?"), followed by the answers, not a narrated summary.
- Limit each episode to a small number of key ideas (about three) so they can all be cycled through later episodes; the retrieval bank grows with the series.
- A short standalone "quiz episode" every few episodes (cumulative, picture-and-speech only) is a cheap way to add a relearning session.
- Encourage rewatching: it is a legitimate spacing event, and for this audience repetition does not appear to cost attention.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Cepeda et al. (2008). My unverified recollection is that the optimal gap is roughly 10-20% of the desired retention interval for delays of weeks and a smaller fraction for a year; check before citing.
- No study was found on opening-question spacing across a video series specifically, or on forgetting curves for video content in dyslexic children.
- No direct experimental comparison of "summary recap" vs "retrieval recap" at the end of a video was found; the recommendation rests on the general testing-effect literature.

## 4. Mnemonics and memory aids for weaker readers

### Takeaway
Picture-based keyword mnemonics have the largest reported effects in the special-education literature (often above 1 SD for students with learning disabilities), but they work for discrete facts and vocabulary, are usually designed by the teacher, and are rated low for general utility. Stories help memory broadly (g about 0.5); songs help word-for-word sequences somewhat and inconsistently.

### Cited Findings
- Scruggs & Mastropieri's early synthesis of mnemonic instruction for students with learning disabilities reported a combined effect of 1.62 SD. Secondary (bibliographic listing; older, largely the authors' own studies). — [Mnemonic Instruction for Students with Learning Disabilities (record)](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/mnemonic-instruction-for-students-with-learning-disabilities-what-it-is-and-what-it-does/812107334636535809-5141)
- Scruggs, Mastropieri, Berkeley & Graetz (2010): 68 investigations, 2,514 students, 80% with learning disabilities; effect sizes for content-area interventions ranged from 0.48 to 1.68, with mnemonics among the strongest. Secondary (as cited in a later article). — [SAGE article citing the 2010 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/0741932513503557)
- The Council for Exceptional Children practice alert describes mnemonic instruction as highly effective for students with mild disabilities across content areas and grade levels. — [CEC Current Practice Alert: Mnemonic Instruction](https://exceptionalchildren.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/original_Alert5.pdf)
- Counterpoint: Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text as low utility for general use. — [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)
- Multimedia pre-training (teaching the names and characteristics of key parts before the explanation) helped in 10 of 10 tests, median d = 0.78; it may not apply when the material is simple or the pace slow. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Narrative: Mar, Li, Nguyen & Ta (2021), 150 effect sizes, 33,078 participants: stories are remembered and understood better than expository text, g = 0.55 (95% CI 0.31-0.79); memory g = 0.72, comprehension g = 0.48; listening g = 0.61 (only 8 studies) vs reading 0.54; under-16s g = 0.67 vs adults 0.44 (differences not significant). Egger's test indicates publication bias, only 28% of effects controlled content, and the authors warn against forcing all content into story form. Verified. — [Mar et al. 2021, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577)
- In science instruction specifically, narrative vs expository materials gave a much smaller advantage, g = 0.16. Secondary. — [Instructional Science 2025 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-025-09723-w)
- Songs: Wallace (1994) found sung text recalled better than spoken only when the melody repeated across verses; Rainey & Larsen (2002) found no faster learning with a familiar melody but easier relearning a week later. Secondary; verify source. — [Review in Chinese Journal of Applied Linguistics](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/cjal-2016-0004/html)
- Knott & Thaut (2018), children aged 9-11: musical mnemonics beat spoken rehearsal, with the advantage reduced to 17% after a 15-minute delay. — [Knott & Thaut 2018, Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2018.00031/pdf)
- A review of science songs calls the learning evidence preliminary. — [Crowther 2012, CBE-Life Sciences Education](https://lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.11-08-0068)

### Inferences
- For a hard new term, show a picture keyword that sounds like the term interacting with its meaning, and say the link aloud. This is the format with the strongest special-education evidence, and it is purely visual-plus-audio. Reserve it for the one or two terms per episode that are truly arbitrary (names, vocabulary), since mnemonics do not build understanding.
- Then retrieve the mnemonic in the pause ("What was the picture for...?"), because mnemonic effects are mostly measured short-term.
- Introduce names of key parts with labelled pictures before the explanation (pre-training), which reduces working-memory load during the explanation itself.
- Wrap the explanation in a small causal story where the content is the plot, not decoration. The science-instruction figure (g = 0.16) suggests the story advantage shrinks when the story is bolted on.
- A short repeated jingle is reasonable for fixed sequences (order of planets, a spelling rule) and should keep the same melody each time; do not expect it to carry conceptual understanding.

### Gaps
- No evidence was gathered in this session on the method of loci or chunking in children or in dyslexia; they are omitted rather than asserted.
- I found no meta-analysis of songs for learning school content.
- Durability of mnemonic gains over weeks in dyslexic learners was not established from the sources reached.
- The 1.62 figure and the 2010 range come from secondary listings and need checking against the original papers.

## 5. Keeping viewers watching: video length, pacing, hooks, and where retention tricks conflict with learning

### Takeaway
The solid findings are: short segments, spoken words with matching pictures, conversational human narration, signalling, and cutting everything decorative. The "6-minute rule" is a correlational MOOC finding about adults that is contested, and YouTube hook advice is practitioner lore; some of it (curiosity gaps) aligns with learning research, while fast cutting, irrelevant spectacle and unrelated jokes work against it.

### Cited Findings

**Length**
- Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014): 6.9 million viewing sessions in four edX courses; median engagement time was near 100% for videos under 6 minutes, about 50% for 9-12 minutes and about 20% for 12-40 minutes; engagement rose with speaking rate; informal talking-head and tablet-drawing videos were more engaging than recorded lectures. — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014, L@S](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf); numbers as summarized in [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380)
- Critique: normalized engagement for 6-9-minute videos was about the same as for 3-6-minute videos, so the data do not single out six minutes. — [Education Week, 2014](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/opinion-short-videos-are-better-for-learning-right-maybe-not/2014/03)
- A later conference paper is titled "The Myth of the Six-Minute Rule" (Lagerstrom, Johanes & Ponsukcharoen 2015). I could not open it. — [ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/the-myth-of-the-six-minute-rule-student-engagement-with-online-videos)
- Guo's data measure watching time in adult volunteer learners, not learning.

**Design principles with effect sizes (Mayer 2023, verified)**
- Coherence (delete extraneous material): 18 of 19 tests, d = 0.86; strongest for learners with low working-memory capacity, system-paced lessons, and highly interesting extraneous material. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Signalling (highlight essentials; outline, vocal stress, arrows, spotlight): 26 of 28, d = 0.70; verbal signalling 0.69, visual 0.71; strongest for low-knowledge learners and when used sparingly. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Modality (spoken rather than printed words with graphics): 18 of 19, d = 1.00, especially when the lesson is fast-paced and words are familiar. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Temporal contiguity (narration at the same moment as the matching picture): 8 of 8, d = 1.31. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Redundancy (do not add full captions to narrated graphics): 8 of 12, d = 0.10, i.e. weak; can reverse when on-screen text is short or the lesson is slow or learner-controlled. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Multimedia (words plus graphics vs words alone): 13 comparisons, median d = 1.35. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Brame (2016) turns these into a checklist: signalling, segmenting, weeding (remove music and busy backgrounds), matching modality, conversational style, enthusiasm, guiding questions, embedded questions. — [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380)

**Where retention tactics conflict with learning**
- Seductive details (interesting but irrelevant material): a 2020 meta-analysis of 58 studies found learners given them scored lower; the effect was larger when the extra material sat next to relevant diagrams and in short lessons. Rey (2012) found stronger harm under limited study time. Secondary (press and review summaries). — [ScienceDaily, Mar 2020](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200319103223.htm); [Learning and the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/); [2023 paper citing both meta-analyses](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-023-09632-w)
- Pace: Lillard & Peterson (2011), 60 four-year-olds: nine minutes of a fast-paced fantastical cartoon produced worse executive-function scores immediately afterwards than a slower educational cartoon or drawing. Only 4-year-olds, immediate effects only, and pace was confounded with fantastical content. — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/674899)
- Blue's Clues deliberately minimized editing and montage so children spend effort on the content rather than on decoding transitions. — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/watch-and-learn)
- Speed: adults lose little comprehension up to 2x playback and lose beyond 2x; tested only with undergraduates and 14-minute lectures on accessible topics. — [Murphy et al. 2022](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)
- Brame's table gives 185-254 words per minute as a speaking-rate guideline derived from Guo's engagement data. This is an engagement correlate in adult MOOC learners, not a comprehension finding. — [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380)
- Misconceptions: Muller, Bewes, Sharma & Reimann (2008), 364 first-year physics students, four versions of a mechanics video: versions that stated and refuted common misconceptions (Refutation, Dialogue) produced larger gains than clear exposition, effect sizes 0.79 and 0.83. An earlier study found d = 0.71 for a dialogue version. Clear, concise exposition felt easier but taught less. — [Muller et al. 2008, abstract](https://perticles.blogspot.com/2008/01/muller-sharma-reimann-jcal-2008.html)

**Creator advice (not peer-reviewed)**
- YouTube Studio reports an "Intro" metric (share of viewers still watching at 30 seconds) and flags spikes and dips; creators advise a hook of roughly 10-15 seconds that proves the title's promise, says why it matters and what is coming, then "re-hooks" between sections. — [1of10 guide](https://1of10.com/blog/how-to-hook-viewers-in-the-first-30-seconds-of-a-youtube-video); [OutlierKit guide](https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-hooks-and-retention/)
- Curiosity-gap advice for informative videos: every answer should raise the next question; examples drawn from Kurzgesagt and Veritasium. — [Descript, The YouTuber's guide to the curiosity gap](https://descript.com/blog/article/the-youtubers-guide-to-the-curiosity-gap-how-to-keep-your-audiences-interest)
- Kurzgesagt describes a script-first process with heavy research and published sources. — [Kurzgesagt, Our video process](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)
- These sources are marketing blogs and creator pages; benchmark numbers in them are unverified.

### Inferences
- Target length: one idea cluster per video, a few minutes long, internally cut into 60-120-second segments by the question pauses. The evidence supports "short and segmented"; it does not support a specific number of minutes for children with dyslexia.
- Narration pace for this audience should be slower than the adult MOOC range. The 185-254 wpm figure is about adults choosing to keep watching; learners with weak phonological processing and working memory need processing time, and the segmenting and coherence effects are strongest exactly when lessons are fast and working memory is low. This is an inference; no direct wpm study for dyslexic children was found.
- Opening 10-15 seconds can serve both retention and learning if the hook is the prediction question about the core idea (a genuine open loop that the content closes). A hook built on an unrelated spectacle is a seductive detail.
- "Pattern interrupts" should be content events: the question pause, a reveal, a change from explaining to doing. Cuts, sound effects and zooms that carry no information are extraneous load.
- Compatible with both goals: curiosity gap, direct address, promise-then-payoff, re-hook questions between segments, stating a common wrong idea and then overturning it.
- In conflict with learning: background music under narration, rapid montage, irrelevant fun facts, on-screen jokes next to diagrams, decorative animation, long branded intros.
- On-screen text: one to three keywords shown exactly when spoken is supported (signalling) and is not what the redundancy principle warns about; full-sentence captions burned into the picture are the thing to avoid by default for weak readers (optional subtitles can still be offered).

### Gaps
- No engagement-by-length data for school-age children or dyslexic learners was found.
- I could not read Guo et al.'s limitations section or the Lagerstrom paper in full.
- I could not verify the pooled effect size of Sundararajan & Adesope (2020); my unverified recollection is about g = -0.33.
- No controlled study was found of YouTube-style hooks, open loops or pattern interrupts on learning outcomes.
- Geri et al. (2017) on video length was not found.

## 6. Playful learning: story, curiosity, humour, characters, game elements, emotional design

### Takeaway
Curiosity before an answer, a causal story, a warm conversational voice, a character that gestures, friendly visuals and light game framing each show small-to-moderate benefits (roughly d = 0.25-0.6, with personalization reported higher); they help when they are tied to the content and hurt when they are added beside it.

### Cited Findings

**Curiosity**
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014): adults saw a trivia question, waited 14 seconds, then saw the answer. High- vs low-curiosity answers were recalled 70.6% vs 54.1% immediately and 45.9% vs 28.1% after one day. Unrelated faces shown during the high-curiosity wait were also remembered slightly better (42.4% vs 38.2%); that incidental benefit was small and, after a day, held only for confident recognitions. Verified. Small adult samples (about 18-28). — [Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014, Neuron](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494)
- Bull & Dizney (1973), as reviewed: prequestions that created a conceptual conflict ("If teachers are seen as middle class, why did high-caste families send their daughters to be teachers?") improved learning, while low-conflict versions of the same question did not. Loewenstein (1994): curiosity arises from a perceived gap in one's knowledge, which presupposes some prior knowledge. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)

**Story**
- Stories are remembered better than exposition (g = 0.55; memory g = 0.72), with caveats on publication bias and content control. — [Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577)
- Willingham describes stories as "psychologically privileged" and gives the working ingredients as causality, conflict (a character with a goal and an obstacle), complications and character. — [Willingham 2004, American Educator](https://www.aft.org/periodical/american-educator/summer-2004/ask-cognitive-scientist)

**Voice, address and characters (Mayer 2023, verified)**
- Personalization (conversational "you/your" wording): 13 of 15 tests, d = 1.00; may not hold for experienced learners or long lessons. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Voice (appealing human voice vs machine voice): 6 of 7, d = 0.74; newer text-to-speech narrows the gap (Craig & Schroeder 2017, 2018). — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Embodiment (on-screen agent that gestures, moves, looks, points; instructor drawing live): 16 of 17, d = 0.58. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Image principle: a static picture of the speaker or character on screen does not reliably help (7 tests, 3 negative, median d = 0.19); "voice may be critical, whereas the image of the agent is not." — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

**Emotional design**
- Brom, Stárková & D'Mello (2018), 33 samples, N = 2,924: adding faces to objects and pleasant colours gave retention d = 0.387, comprehension 0.317, transfer 0.327, intrinsic motivation 0.255, lower perceived difficulty (-0.208), and only a marginal effect on positive affect (0.113). — [Brom et al. 2018 (author manuscript)](https://artemis.ms.mff.cuni.cz/main/papers/emodesign_manuscript_1rev_180928_FINAL_norev.pdf)
- Wong & Adesope (2021): retention g = 0.35, transfer 0.27, comprehension 0.29, intrinsic motivation 0.15. Secondary. — [Wong & Adesope 2021 (abstract)](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2020/journal-article-abstracts/s10648-020-09545-x/)
- A separate meta-analysis reports retention d = 0.25 and a larger rise in positive emotion (d = 0.64). — [Psychological Development and Education 2019](https://devpsy.bnu.edu.cn/EN/Y2019/V35/I6/697)

**Gamification**
- Sailer & Homner (2020), 38 publications: small positive effects on cognitive (g = 0.49), motivational (0.36) and behavioural (0.25) outcomes; only the cognitive effect was stable in high-rigour studies; game fiction (a story frame) and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes. Effect sizes secondary; abstract verified. — [Sailer & Homner 2020, Educational Psychology Review](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)

**Humour**
- Instructional Humor Processing Theory predicts, and reviews generally find, that humour related to the content and non-aggressive helps retention, while unrelated or disparaging humour does not. A five-decade review notes inconsistent results; much of the evidence uses perceived rather than measured learning. — [National Communication Association summary](https://natcom.org/node/2520); [SAGE Open 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2158244016670200)

**Where playfulness harms**
- Interesting-but-irrelevant additions reduce learning, most for learners with low working-memory capacity. — [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Fast-paced fantastical cartoons depressed 4-year-olds' executive function immediately afterwards. — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/674899)

### Inferences
- The question-wait-answer rhythm is itself the play mechanic: a riddle with a short suspense gap. Gruber's 14-second wait shows that a pause before the answer is not dead time when the viewer wants to know.
- Make the prediction question a small puzzle with a surprising resolution, ideally one that exposes a common wrong belief (Muller; Bull & Dizney). "Most people guess X" is both a hook and a way to make wrong answers normal.
- Give the series a recurring character who has a goal and gets things wrong first. The character must do something (point, gesture, draw, react); a mascot sitting in the corner is the image principle, not the embodiment principle.
- Narrate in second person, informal, with a real or high-quality natural voice.
- Use round, friendly shapes, faces on the objects being explained and warm colours; expect a modest gain and lower perceived difficulty, not a transformation.
- Game framing that fits a plain video: a mission or mystery per episode (game fiction), "beat your own score" counts ("How many of the three did you get?"), collectable story progress across the series. Avoid leaderboards, timers and public comparison for this audience.
- Jokes should be about the content (a funny wrong prediction, an absurd consequence of the rule). Cut jokes that need reading, and cut running gags unrelated to the idea.
- A simple test for any playful element: if it were removed, would the explanation lose something? If not, it is a seductive detail.

### Gaps
- I did not find a source in this session that defines what makes an activity feel like play rather than a lesson (autonomy, low stakes, surprise); self-determination theory and playful-learning frameworks were not searched, so that part of the brief rests on inference.
- Um, Plass, Hayward & Homer (2012) was not opened directly; it is represented through the meta-analyses that include it.
- No pooled effect size for related vs unrelated humour was found.
- Loewenstein (1994) was accessed only as described in a later review.
- No study of emotional design, mascots or gamification specifically in dyslexic learners was found.

## 7. Motivation, tone and feedback for learners with dyslexia

### Takeaway
Poor readers carry measurably more anxiety (about d = 0.4) and a specifically academic, not global, drop in self-concept, so questions must be visibly stake-free, speech-based, winnable and never shaming; done that way, frequent quizzing tends to lower anxiety rather than raise it.

### Cited Findings
- Francis, Caruana, Hudson & McArthur (2019), 34 studies, 16,275 participants (2,491 poor readers): poor readers show more internalising problems (d = 0.41), anxiety (d = 0.41) and depression (d = 0.23). Correlational; the direction of causation is open. — [Francis et al. 2019, Clinical Psychology Review (record)](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8z3w0/the-association-between-poor-reading-and-internalising-problems-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)
- The difficulties are domain-specific: in 124 children aged 8-11, those with dyslexia had more anxiety and lower self-esteem in the school domain, while general anxiety and self-esteem were not impaired. — [Novita 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Shally-Novita/publication/290248348_Secondary_symptoms_of_dyslexia_a_comparison_of_self-esteem_and_anxiety_profiles_of_children_with_and_without_dyslexia/links/56cc190708ae1106370cc42d/Secondary-symptoms-of-dyslexia-a-comparison-of-self-esteem-and-anxiety-profiles-of-children-with-and-without-dyslexia.pdf)
- Children with dyslexia usually report negative academic self-concept, not lower general self-concept (McArthur et al. 2021, as cited), and higher anxiety than peers. — [Frontiers in Education 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1371627/text)
- Teaching that leans on reading and writing produces frustration and anxiety in dyslexic learners (Carroll & Iles 2006), and dyslexia often comes with working-memory limitations; replacing typed responses with spoken ones removed most of the performance gap in retrieval practice. — [Wilschut, Sense & van Rijn 2024](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/57w2x9vr)
- Agarwal et al. (2014), survey of 1,408 middle- and high-school students after classroom retrieval practice: 72% said it made them less nervous for tests, 22% the same, 6% more nervous; 92% said it helped them learn. Self-report, not measured anxiety. — [Agarwal et al. 2014 (record)](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/classroom-based-programs-of-retrieval-practice-reduce-middle-scho/); [retrievalpractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/reduce-anxiety)
- Interpolated quizzing reduced anxiety about a later test and lowered perceived mental demand in adults. — [Szpunar, Khan & Schacter 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)
- Counter-evidence: highly test-anxious students sometimes avoid retrieval practice altogether (Liu et al. 2024, secondary), and some students find embedded quizzes anxiety-provoking. — [retrievalpractice.org](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/reduce-anxiety); [embedded-quiz study, TechTrends 2019 (attribution not verified)](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11528-019-00374-6)
- Retrieval works in children only when initial success is high (75-87% with cues vs no benefit at 8%). — [Karpicke, Blunt & Smith 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786565)
- The Learning Scientists' classroom guidance: respond kindly to wrong answers, correct positively and give the right answer. — [The Learning Scientists, Feb 2024](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/2/22)
- Emotional design lowers perceived difficulty (d = -0.208). — [Brom et al. 2018](https://artemis.ms.mff.cuni.cz/main/papers/emodesign_manuscript_1rev_180928_FINAL_norev.pdf)

### Inferences
- Never call it a test or quiz. Frame pauses as "your turn", "guess", "mission". The anxiety benefit of retrieval practice depends on it being experienced as low-stakes.
- No visible countdown that implies failure when it runs out; use a calm progress cue and say "pause me if you want more time". Time pressure is exactly where slow phonological retrieval gets punished.
- Design for about 80% success: cue the answer, offer picture choices, ask for one word. Early wins rebuild the sense that "I can do school things", which is the specific deficit (academic self-concept).
- Feedback wording that cannot shame: give the answer, attribute errors to the trickiness of the idea or to how common the mistake is, and immediately show why. Do not use buzzers, red crosses or "wrong".
- Praise effort and strategy sparingly and specifically; uncontingent "Great job!" after every pause is not credible in a non-interactive video.
- Show mastery building: the "last time" questions at the start of each episode are chances to notice "I still know this". Say so explicitly.
- Make speech the default response. For this audience it is both the accessible mode and the one with direct evidence of benefit.
- Keep any game element private and self-referenced.

### Gaps
- No experimental study was found on feedback tone or wording for dyslexic learners in video or quiz settings; the recommendations above combine the anxiety/self-concept literature with general retrieval-practice findings.
- I could not verify a McArthur et al. meta-analysis of self-concept in poor readers or its effect size; it appears only as a citation in a 2024 paper.
- No evidence was found on whether timed vs untimed pauses change anxiety or learning in dyslexic children.
- Growth-mindset or attribution-retraining evidence for this population was not searched.
