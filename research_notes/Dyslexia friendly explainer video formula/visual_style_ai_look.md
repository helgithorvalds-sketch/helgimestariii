# Visual design of educational explainer videos: which styles teach best, are enjoyable, and how to avoid the generic "AI video" look

Researcher notes, compiled 19 Nov 2026. Evidence tags used throughout: **[STRONG]** = meta-analysis or several converging peer-reviewed studies; **[MODERATE]** = single or few peer-reviewed studies, or mixed results; **[PRACTITIONER]** = creator/industry statement or opinion; **[VENDOR]** = survey or study from a company with a commercial interest. "(abstract/secondary)" means I only saw the abstract or a secondary summary, not the full text — verify numbers before quoting them in a final report.

---

## 1. 2D vs 3D vs live action vs whiteboard/hand-drawn: what does the research say about learning outcomes?

### Takeaway
No rendering style wins by itself. Animation beats static pictures only modestly overall (g ≈ 0.2–0.4), and the gain is concentrated where the motion itself *is* the content (processes, change over time, procedures) and where the animation is representational rather than decorative. 3D helps mainly for inherently spatial content and can overload low-spatial-ability learners; hand-drawn / whiteboard-style build-up has the best combined evidence for enjoyment plus learning at low cost, so for a solo creator a simple 2D, drawn-looking style is the evidence-backed default, with 3D reserved for a few spatial topics.

### Cited Findings

**Animation vs static graphics**
- **[STRONG]** Höffler & Leutner (2007) meta-analysis: 26 studies, 76 pairwise comparisons; overall advantage of animation over static pictures d = 0.37 (95% CI 0.25–0.49). Larger when the animation is *representational* rather than decorational (d = 0.40), when it is highly realistic/video-based (d = 0.76), and for procedural-motor knowledge (d = 1.06). — [Leibniz IPN publication record](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/instructional-animation-versus-static-pictures-a-meta-analysis); [full paper PDF](https://wikicap.ulb.be/images/e/ef/Hoffler_Leutner_2007.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** Berney & Bétrancourt (2016) meta-analysis (140 pairwise comparisons): smaller overall effect, Hedges's g = 0.226 (95% CI 0.12–0.33). Moderators: system-paced animation g = 0.309; animation with *auditory commentary* g = 0.336; instruction *without accompanying text* g = 0.883. — [Univ. of Geneva archive record](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:92234); [EARLI summary PDF](https://tecfa.unige.ch/perso/sandra/pdf/Earli2016_berney_betrancourt_FINAL.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** Castro-Alonso et al. (2019) meta-analysis: 46 studies, 82 effect sizes, 5,474 participants; dynamic visualisations beat static by a small margin (g+ = 0.23). — [Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09469-1) (abstract/secondary)
- **[MODERATE]** Ploetzner et al. (2021): three meta-analyses together suggest the animation advantage is "rather limited overall", but a re-analysis shows animations are considerably more effective when learners must learn *the specifics of the displayed change* (i.e. how something moves/transforms). — [Instructional Science](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-021-09541-w)
- **[STRONG, theory + review]** Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt (2002), "Animation: can it facilitate?": where animation looked superior, the animated version often simply carried more information or interactivity. Two principles: the **Congruence Principle** (the format of the graphic should match the structure of the concept — animation suits change over time) and the **Apprehension Principle** (graphics must be accurately perceived — animations are "often too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived"; many continuous events are conceived as discrete steps). — [Tversky et al. 2002 PDF (Stanford course copy)](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)

**Realistic vs schematic**
- **[MODERATE, mixed]** The "schematic beats realistic" claim is real but not universal. A 2021 review (Skulmowski et al.) cites Scheiter et al. (2009) and Menendez et al. (2020) as studies where abstract/schematic visualisations produced better learning than realistic ones, with unnecessary realistic detail adding cognitive load, but concludes that a higher degree of realism is not detrimental per se — conditions matter. — [Educational Psychology Review 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09638-1) (abstract/secondary)
- **[MODERATE]** Realism can help when the *shape/appearance itself* must be remembered: a 2024 study found a more realistic visualisation beat a schematic one in an anatomy task; a 2022 study concluded the optimum may lie toward the realistic end for memorising shapes tested with image-based tests; and test format matters (realistic tests favour those who studied realistic images). — [Cognitive Processing 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10339-024-01183-3); [Education and Information Technologies 2022](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10639-022-11043-2) (abstract/secondary)
- Note the tension with Höffler & Leutner's d = 0.76 for "highly realistic, video-based" animations: that moderator mostly reflects procedural/motor tasks shown on video, not glossy rendering of concepts. — [Höffler & Leutner PDF](https://wikicap.ulb.be/images/e/ef/Hoffler_Leutner_2007.pdf)

**3D vs 2D and spatial ability**
- **[MODERATE]** Huk (2006), "Who benefits from learning with 3D models? The case of spatial ability" (J. Computer Assisted Learning 22, 392–404): only students with *high* spatial ability benefited from interactive 3D models; low-spatial-ability students were cognitively overloaded. — [Scilit record](https://www.scilit.net/publications/eef4277a964ed2b221b506ef4e15e3e1) (secondary summary)
- **[MODERATE, mixed]** In anatomy (the best-studied 3D domain): a meta-analysis (Yammine & Violato) found a positive effect of 3D visualisation on anatomy learning, but sources disagree on which sub-effect (factual vs spatial knowledge) is 0.30 vs 0.50; a review of digital 3D anatomy tools found 12 studies where 3D was significantly better than traditional methods and 9 where it was not; monoscopic (screen-displayed) 3D may significantly *disadvantage* students with lower visual-spatial ability; a meta-analysis of 13 RCTs found stereoscopic vision added nothing for *non-interactive* 3D images. — [PMC6916638](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6916638) and [Leiden thesis chapters](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3274196/view) (secondary summary)
- **[MODERATE]** Spatial ability does not always moderate: a 2020 study on star motions found spatial ability helped overall but did not moderate dynamic-vs-static effects. — [NCU scholars record](https://scholars.ncu.edu.tw/en/publications/the-effects-of-visualization-format-and-spatial-ability-on-learni/) (abstract)

**Whiteboard / hand-drawn / instructor-drawn**
- **[MODERATE]** Türkay & Mouton (2016): online experiment, 568 MTurk participants, whiteboard animations vs other common formats — whiteboard animation helped learning and gave higher self-reported enjoyment and engagement. — [Harvard VPAL record](https://www.vpal.harvard.edu/publications/educational-impact-whiteboard-animations-experiment-using-popular-social-science)
- **[MODERATE]** Türkay (2016, Computers & Education): with physics topics, whiteboard animation gave significantly better retention than audio-only and text presentations (as cited by a 2023 study). — [Smart Learning Environments 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s40561-023-00258-6); [paper listing](https://cs.reviewer.ly/app/publication/698b0518-9265-4e9d-97d8-0c07c148a943)
- **[MODERATE]** Türkay (2022, AJET): 299 participants, philosophy and psychology lessons, four formats; whiteboard animation and audio-only groups reported more enjoyment and engagement. The enjoyment/engagement finding is more consistent across these studies than the retention finding. — [AJET](https://ajet.org.au/index.php/AJET/article/view/7639)
- **[MODERATE]** Fiorella & Mayer (2016), J. Educational Psychology 108, 528–546: five experiments on a short Doppler-effect lesson; watching the instructor *draw* diagrams while explaining beat already-drawn diagrams; Fiorella suggests the visible hand may be a key factor (social cue plus attention guidance) but cautions against deriving principles from short lab studies of a single topic. — [Fiorella guest post, Learning Scientists](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2017/1/24-1); [UCSB thesis record](https://alexandria.ucsb.edu/lib/ark:/48907/f37d2sbv)
- **[MODERATE]** Fiorella, Stull, Kuhlmann & Mayer (2020), kidney lesson: asking learners to explain was most effective for those who watched *instructor-generated (dynamically drawn)* visuals (ds > 1.0) rather than static visuals (ds < 0.5). Ongoing work tests whether instructor drawing helps mainly by guiding attention. — [ECR Hub record](https://ecrhub.org/publications/fostering-generative-learning-video-lessons-benefits-instructor-generated-drawings-and); [UGA project note](https://oibr.uga.edu/advancing-evidence-based-video-instruction-in-stem-learning)
- **[STRONG for engagement, not learning]** Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014): 6.9 million edX video-watching sessions; "Khan-style tablet drawing tutorials are more engaging than PowerPoint slides or code screencasts"; shorter videos much more engaging — segment into chunks under 6 minutes. Observational; engagement is "necessary but not sufficient" for learning. — [Guo et al. 2014 PDF](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)

**Live action**
- **[STRONG for procedures]** Video-based/realistic depiction is the largest moderator in Höffler & Leutner (d = 0.76) and procedural-motor knowledge shows d = 1.06 — live footage is the right tool for "how to physically do X" (experiments, craft, sport), not for abstract mechanisms. — [Höffler & Leutner PDF](https://wikicap.ulb.be/images/e/ef/Hoffler_Leutner_2007.pdf)

### Inferences
- **2D is the right default; "2D vs 3D" is the wrong first question.** The moderators that matter are: (a) is the visual representational of the mechanism, (b) does motion depict a real change, (c) is it paced so it can be perceived, (d) is it narrated rather than text-explained. All of these are style-independent and cheap in 2D.
- **Narrated animation with little on-screen text is the best-supported configuration** (Berney & Bétrancourt's "no accompanying text" g = 0.883 and "auditory commentary" g = 0.336) — which happens to be exactly the configuration a dyslexic audience needs.
- **Use 3D only when the third dimension is the thing being taught** (organ positions, molecular shape, orbits/tilt/seasons, map projections, geometry of solids), and even then prefer a slow, system-controlled rotation of a simple shaded model over a glossy fly-through; the Huk result suggests weaker spatial learners can be *hurt* by 3D. Everything else rendered in 3D is costly decoration. A cheap compromise: 2D with a few "2.5D" shots (Kurzgesagt mixes "classic 2D flat animation with 3D elements" — see section 3).
- **Progressive "being drawn" build-up is the cheapest high-evidence motion.** It reproduces the Türkay / Fiorella & Mayer conditions (visual appears in sync with speech, attention is led to one new element at a time) and satisfies Tversky's apprehension principle by turning a continuous process into discrete steps. A line-draw/reveal animation can be done programmatically (SVG path drawing) without any AI video model. Whether a literal hand must be visible is unresolved; attention guidance is the leading candidate mechanism, which a moving pen-tip/cursor/highlight may also deliver.
- **Schematic-leaning, not photoreal.** For mechanisms (how a volcano erupts, how a fraction works) simplified visuals carry less extraneous detail; add realism only when learners must later *recognise the real thing* (leaf shapes, rock types, a real map).

### Gaps
- I could not verify Scheiter et al. (2009) or Butcher (2006) directly, nor find a dedicated meta-analysis of realism vs schematic visuals; the "schematic often wins" claim rests on individual studies and one review.
- Höffler (2010), "Spatial ability: its influence on learning with visualizations — a meta-analytic review" surfaced only as a title ([record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/spatial-ability-its-influence-on-learning-with-visualizations-a-meta-analytic-review/811792669750591488-10463)); I did not confirm its numbers (commonly cited as showing dynamic and 3D visualisations partly compensate for low spatial ability — which would *conflict* with Huk 2006; treat the spatial-ability direction as unsettled).
- Fiorella & Mayer (2016) effect sizes per experiment were not retrieved.
- No study found that compares 2D vs 3D vs whiteboard styles head-to-head specifically for learners with dyslexia, nor for Icelandic-language audiences.
- Almost all of this research uses short lessons (minutes), immediate tests, and university students; generalisation to school-age dyslexic learners is an assumption.

## 2. Explanatory vs decorative visuals: why generic icons fail and what good explanatory visuals do

### Takeaway
A visual teaches only when it carries the explanation (structure, change, cause and effect); pictures that merely decorate or label what is being said give no benefit and can actively harm learning. The evidence-backed toolkit is: make the picture represent the mechanism, build it step by step in sync with the narration, cue the one thing being talked about, and strip everything else.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** Representational vs decorational is a moderator in the animation literature: the animation benefit is d = 0.40 when the animation is representational of the content. — [Höffler & Leutner 2007](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/instructional-animation-versus-static-pictures-a-meta-analysis)
- **[STRONG]** Seductive-details meta-analysis (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, "Keep it Coherent", Educational Psychology Review 32(3), 707–734): 58 studies, >7,500 students; learners given interesting-but-irrelevant material scored lower. Harm was worse when the seductive detail sat next to relevant diagrams and when it was constant (e.g. a static joke or image left on screen). Most studies used 6–12 minute sessions; only cognitive outcomes were measured (emotional benefits not captured). — [WSU news summary](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [paper record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Keep-it-Coherent-A-Meta-Analysis-of/99900601155501842)
- **[STRONG]** Signaling/cueing meta-analysis (Alpizar, Adesope & Wong 2020): 29 studies, 44 effect sizes, 2,726 participants; signaling improves learning, d = 0.38; stronger in shorter interventions and when the signals are paired with visuals. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842) (abstract/secondary)
- **[STRONG]** Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016) meta-analysis of signals that link words and pictures: r = 0.17 (95% CI .11–.22) for transfer/comprehension, especially helpful for learners with **low prior knowledge**. — [Meta-analysis record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/signaling-text-picture-relations-in-multimedia-learning-a-comprehensive-meta-analysis/814569585163370497-11949); [data/code archive](https://psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.12034/2018)
- **[STRONG, theory]** Tversky's Congruence Principle: the structure of the graphic should correspond to the structure of the concept (motion for change over time, spatial arrangement for relations); Apprehension Principle: break continuous events into perceivable discrete steps. — [Tversky et al. 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)
- **[MODERATE]** Animation helps most when "the specifics of the displayed changes need to be learned" — i.e. motion should be reserved for showing how something actually changes. — [Ploetzner et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-021-09541-w)
- **[MODERATE]** Clear, fluent explanation is not enough if the learner holds a misconception: Derek Muller's (Veritasium) PhD research found "clear, concise" expository science videos made students feel confident without learning; a video that first raised the common misconception nearly doubled post-test scores (6.0 → 11 of 26), attributed to increased mental effort. — [Open Culture summary of Muller's video](https://www.openculture.com/2012/06/expert_gently_asks_whether_khan_academy_videos_promote_meaningful_learning.html) (secondary)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Kurzgesagt: illustration starts only after the script; "after sketching, the team settles on visual metaphors and ideas for each scene". — [Kurzgesagt process page](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** TED-Ed: for tectonic plates, animator Biljana Labovic chose a physical object that could itself move and shift as the visual metaphor ("visualizing big ideas" episode). — [TED-Ed season 0 listing](https://bingebase.com/tv/ted-ed-2012/season/0)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Numberblocks: the picture *is* the concept — each character is built from that many blocks; "when One joins Two, they become Three"; Nine is three rows of three; Ten is a 1 and a 0. — [Numberblocks "How it works"](https://www.blocksuniverse.tv/numberblocks/how-it-works)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Vox video (Joss Fong): choose stories "poorly suited for text articles ... specifically better in video"; Cleo Abram (ex-Vox) describes looking for "the visual that helps viewers understand it". — [Nieman Lab on Vox video](https://www.niemanlab.org/?p=124626); [Video Consortium interview, Cleo Abram](https://videoconsortium.org/mag/episode-8-cleo-abram) (search-summary level; quotes not checked against full text)

### Inferences
- **Diagnosis of the creator's failed attempts.** Generic flat icons are "decorational" in Höffler & Leutner's sense: an icon of a lightbulb, a heart or a book names a noun the narration already says. It adds no relation, no change and no cause, so it cannot produce the representational-animation benefit; and when icons sit on screen for a long time beside the real content they behave like the "constant" seductive details that Sundararajan & Adesope found most harmful. This is a sourced explanation of why they "didn't really help".
- **A per-shot test that is cheap to apply to every video:** "If I mute the narration, does this shot still show *what happens* or *how the parts relate*?" If the shot only shows *what the sentence is about*, replace it. Useful shot types (derived from the principles above, not from a single source):
  1. **Mechanism shot** — parts move and cause other parts to change (water heats → rises → cools → falls).
  2. **Build-up diagram** — start empty, add one element per sentence, never more than one new element at a time (Apprehension Principle + whiteboard findings).
  3. **Before/after or comparison** — two states side by side with the single difference highlighted.
  4. **Concrete analogy** — a familiar object that behaves like the abstract thing (pizza slices for fractions; a physical object that shifts for tectonic plates), then map it back explicitly.
  5. **Zoom-in / zoom-out** — keep the whole in view, then zoom to the part being discussed, to preserve context.
  6. **Misconception shot** — show the wrong picture most people have, then break it (Muller).
- **Signaling on every shot:** highlight/colour-pop, arrow, pulse or zoom on the element the narrator is naming at that instant; dim the rest. d ≈ 0.38 and stronger for low-prior-knowledge learners means this is one of the highest-value, lowest-cost habits, and in a code-based pipeline it can be a reusable component.
- **One focal point per shot** follows from apprehension + coherence: every additional moving or decorative element competes for attention. Decorative backgrounds, floating particles and ambient loops should be static or absent while an explanation is running.
- **Consistent visual vocabulary** (same colour always means the same thing, same arrow style always means "causes", same character always stands for the same concept) reduces the amount learners must re-decode each video. This is an inference from signaling and coherence research rather than a directly tested claim.

### Gaps
- Carney & Levin's (2002) taxonomy of picture functions (decorational, representational, organisational, interpretational, transformational) is the classic source for "decorative pictures do nothing"; I could not retrieve it in this session, so it is not cited above.
- The seductive-details meta-analysis effect size itself was not retrieved (direction and moderators only).
- Schneider et al. (2018) signaling meta-analysis: citation found, effect size not retrieved.
- I found no experimental study that specifically tests "generic icon B-roll" against mechanism animations; the conclusion is an application of the decorative/seductive-details literature.
- Evidence for visual metaphor/analogy in video is practitioner-level here; the analogy-learning research literature was not searched.

## 3. What the best explainer channels do, and which styles and themes fit low-reading audiences

### Takeaway
The channels that are both loved and effective share a process, not a look: script first, then one deliberate visual idea per scene, narration timed before animation, a tightly fixed house style, and custom sound. For low-reading audiences the best-fitting models are narration-led formats where the picture carries the reasoning (Kurzgesagt, TED-Ed, 3Blue1Brown, Khan-style drawing) and character-as-concept formats (Numberblocks/Alphablocks); text-heavy or talking-head-with-captions formats fit worst.

### Cited Findings
- **Kurzgesagt [PRACTITIONER, primary]** — Five phases: research (books, papers, expert consultation, a source sheet per video) → script ("about a dozen drafts") → illustration (after sketching, the team "settles on visual metaphors and ideas for each scene"; vector and pixel illustration plus infographics; style described as "playful" with "quirky and relatable characters") → voiceover and animation (the narrator's voiceover "sets the timing for the animation team", who combine "classic 2D flat animation with 3D elements") → specifically composed music and sound effects per video. — [Kurzgesagt, "Our video process"](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)
- **Kurzgesagt [PRACTITIONER, secondary copies of their 2020 video]** — A video takes roughly 1,200 hours end to end; scripts take from a few weeks to years; about 200 panels are illustrated in Adobe Illustrator, mostly from scratch. — [Kottke on "How to Make a Kurzgesagt Video in 1200 Hours"](https://also.kottke.org/20/02/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-just-1200-hours); [Gigazine summary](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20200217-science-animation-making/)
- **3Blue1Brown [PRACTITIONER]** — Visuals are generated predominantly with Manim, Grant Sanderson's own programmatic Python animation library ("all programmatic ... very bespoke"); he drops planned videos when he cannot write a script he finds compelling; ideas evolve from sketches and drawings into a logically coherent video. — [3Blue1Brown FAQ](https://www.3blue1brown.com/faq/); [Wikipedia: 3Blue1Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3Blue1Brown); [Stanford Daily interview](https://stanforddaily.com/2020/01/24/3blue1brown-creator-grant-sanderson-15-talks-engaging-with-math-using-stories-and-visuals/)
- **TED-Ed [PRACTITIONER]** — Educator writes a short 800–900 word script (described as "the hardest part"); TED-Ed fact-checks, then engages a narrator and an animator; the animation director researches the topic visually, develops a style and look, creates characters and storyboards; animation takes roughly one to two months (secondhand figure). Each lesson gets its own art style from a different animator. — [EdSurge, behind the scenes of TED-Ed](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-08-16-behind-the-scenes-of-ted-ed-s-wildly-popular-youtube-channel-for-students); [Animafest lecture note on TED-Ed](https://animafest.hr/en/2015/side_event_pro/read/lecture_ted_ed)
- **Vox [PRACTITIONER]** — Picks stories that are "specifically better in video" than text; a single person owns each video from pitch to publication. — [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/?p=124626); [Fast Company on Vox video](https://www.fastcompany.com/40575395/voxs-netflix-ambitions-are-just-the-beginning) (search-summary level)
- **Veritasium [MODERATE + PRACTITIONER]** — Derek Muller (PhD in physics education research, Univ. of Sydney) structures videos around viewers' misconceptions because plain clear exposition produced confidence without learning in his studies. — [Open Culture summary](https://www.openculture.com/2012/06/expert_gently_asks_whether_khan_academy_videos_promote_meaningful_learning.html)
- **Khan Academy style [STRONG for engagement]** — Tablet-drawing tutorials are more engaging than slides in 6.9M edX sessions; keep segments under 6 minutes. — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- **Numberblocks / Alphablocks (CBeebies, Blue Zoo; creator Joe Elliot) [PRACTITIONER, institution-backed]** — Made with the UK's NCETM; 5-minute episodes, each "built around mathematics rather than added on"; characters' bodies mirror real arithmetic (3 and 2 combine to form 5; 5 can split into 4 and 1); each number has its own colour, features that encode it (Three: three blocks, three juggling balls, three-pointed hat; Six: dice spots) and a personality tied to it (Four is "solid and square"); humour and catchphrases; five colour-coded levels of progression. — [Numberblocks "How it works"](https://www.blocksuniverse.tv/numberblocks/how-it-works); [NCETM director's blog](https://ncetm.org.uk/features/guest-blog-by-debbie-morgan-ncetm-director-for-primary-mathematics)
- **Sesame Street [PRACTITIONER + MODERATE]** — Sesame Workshop's curriculum lead Rosemarie Truglio: learning rides on the child's relationship with the character ("I care about Elmo ... if he likes broccoli, I'm willing to try the broccoli"); research reviews report that strengthening children's parasocial relationship with an educational character boosts learning from that show. — [Edutopia interview](https://www.edutopia.org/article/how-sesame-streets-muppets-became-revolutionaries); [Children and Screens research summary](https://www.childrenandscreens.org/learn-explore/research/young-children-media-characters-and-learning/)
- **Whiteboard/RSA-style [MODERATE]** — Higher enjoyment and engagement than other common formats, with some learning advantage. — [Türkay & Mouton 2016](https://www.vpal.harvard.edu/publications/educational-impact-whiteboard-animations-experiment-using-popular-social-science)

### Inferences
- **Process pattern worth copying (solo-scaled):** (1) script and a one-line "visual idea" per sentence before any asset is generated; (2) record/generate the narration first and time every shot to it (Kurzgesagt explicitly animates to the finished voiceover); (3) one fixed house style reused across all videos (Kurzgesagt, Numberblocks) rather than a new look per video (TED-Ed can afford that only because each video has a dedicated animator for 1–2 months); (4) music and sound effects designed, not default.
- **Fit for low-reading audiences (my ranking, inference):**
  - *Best:* narration-led animation where the picture carries the reasoning and text is nearly absent — Kurzgesagt, TED-Ed, 3Blue1Brown-style programmatic diagrams, Khan-style progressive drawing, MinutePhysics-style stick-figure drawing; and character-as-concept series (Numberblocks/Alphablocks).
  - *Workable with adaptation:* Vox-style (relies on documents, charts and on-screen quotes — needs text stripped); Oversimplified / Simple History-style humorous history cartoons (story- and character-driven, low text, but jokes can become seductive details if they do not carry the content).
  - *Worst fit:* slide/text-heavy explainers, kinetic-typography videos, talking heads where the meaning is carried by captions.
- **Themes/framings that are both fun and explanation-friendly (inference from the above):**
  - *Recurring characters who embody the concept* (Numberblocks principle) — strongest because the character design itself is the diagram; transferable to older audiences as, e.g., a cell organelle crew, grammatical cases as characters with fixed "powers", fractions as pieces of one consistent object.
  - *Mystery / "why does this happen?"* and *misconception-first* openings — backed by Muller's results; give a reason to keep watching and force comparison with the viewer's own belief.
  - *Journey/quest through a system* (follow a water drop, a red blood cell, a letter through the post, a law through parliament) — maps naturally onto step-by-step build-up and one-focal-point-per-shot.
  - *"What if" scenarios* (Kurzgesagt staple) and *everyday-life problems* — anchor abstraction in something concrete.
  - *Humour* — keep it attached to the mechanism (the joke is the misconception breaking, or the character's concept-defined personality) so it is not a seductive detail.
- **Not being Kurzgesagt is fine:** 1,200 hours per video is a team budget. The transferable parts are the script-first discipline and the fixed style, not the polish. A rougher, drawn-looking style with fewer, clearer shots is closer to the whiteboard/Khan/MinutePhysics tradition, which has better direct learning evidence than high-polish motion design anyway.

### Gaps
- No sourced process information found in this session for CrashCourse/Thought Café, MinutePhysics, Oversimplified, Simple History or Osmosis; comments on them above are my own characterisation (inference), not cited fact.
- No interview with Joe Elliot (Numberblocks/Alphablocks creator) was found; Numberblocks evidence is from the producer's and NCETM's own pages (promotional framing, not independent evaluation).
- 3Blue1Brown's exact order of visuals vs script was not confirmed.
- No research found comparing narrative framings (mystery vs quest vs "what if") for learning or enjoyment; the theme ranking is inference.
- Nothing found on explainer-channel preferences among Icelandic learners or among dyslexic teens specifically.

## 4. Character, colour and emotional design

### Takeaway
Giving non-human elements a simple face and using pleasant, warm, saturated colours yields a small-to-medium positive effect on learning and motivation (meta-analytic d ≈ 0.3–0.4), though individual studies sometimes find nothing, and familiar recurring characters help children learn. The evidence supports light anthropomorphism and a warm, consistent palette — applied to the content-bearing elements, not as added decoration — but most studies are on university students or young children, so the age-appropriate middle ground for teens is a design judgement.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** "Emotional design" was introduced by Um, Plass, Hayward & Homer (2012): design features (colour, shape) intended to induce positive emotion to aid learning; Plass et al. (2014, Learning & Instruction) tested shape and colour effects on affect and learning directly. — [Brom et al. 2018 manuscript (background section)](https://artemis.ms.mff.cuni.cz/main/papers/emodesign_manuscript_1rev_180928_FINAL_norev.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** Brom, Stárková & D'Mello (2018) meta-analysis of facial anthropomorphism and pleasant colours: 33 independent samples, N = 2,924; positive effects on retention (k = 18, d+ = 0.387) and comprehension (k = 14, d+ = 0.317). Facial anthropomorphism = adding eyes/mouth to non-human graphic elements. — [Brom et al. 2018 manuscript](https://artemis.ms.mff.cuni.cz/main/papers/emodesign_manuscript_1rev_180928_FINAL_norev.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** Wong & Adesope (2021) replication/extension meta-analysis: retention 0.35, transfer 0.27, comprehension 0.29; also higher intrinsic motivation and lower perceived difficulty — with those affective effects reported as larger for *static* than animated materials. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Meta-Analysis-of-Emotional-Designs-in-Multimedia/99900601054701842); [citing review, Smart Learning Environments 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40561-024-00332-7) (secondary)
- **[MODERATE, counter-evidence]** Results are inconsistent at study level: Heidig et al. (2015) and others found emotional design did not improve retention or transfer, and in one case pleasant colours were null or even harmful. Most people prefer warm, highly saturated, bright colours (preference data, not learning data). A 2024 meta-analysis (Liu & Su) focuses specifically on facial anthropomorphism. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1393113/pdf) (secondary)
- **[MODERATE]** Familiar characters: Calvert and colleagues (2013, Media Psychology) built parasocial relationships between toddlers and media characters to teach early maths; reviews report that characters become meaningful when familiar, likeable and engaged in real-world-like experiences, and that strengthening the parasocial relationship boosts learning from the show. — [Children and Screens summary](https://www.childrenandscreens.org/learn-explore/research/young-children-media-characters-and-learning/)
- **[MODERATE, counter-evidence]** A 2024 study found parasocial relationship strength was high for all character types and did not relate to children's preferences or judgements — "more attachment = more learning" is not a simple rule. — [Frontiers in Developmental Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/developmental-psychology/articles/10.3389/fdpys.2024.1438391/epub)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Character-as-concept with consistent per-character colour and personality (Numberblocks); "quirky and relatable characters" in a uniform playful style used for an all-ages/adult audience (Kurzgesagt). — [Numberblocks](https://www.blocksuniverse.tv/numberblocks/how-it-works); [Kurzgesagt](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)
- **[STRONG, caution]** Irrelevant interesting additions harm learning, worst when constantly present next to the relevant diagram. — [Seductive details meta-analysis summary](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/)

### Inferences
- **Put the face on the thing being explained** (the water molecule, the fraction piece, the verb), not on a separate mascot who stands beside the diagram. That is how the emotional-design studies operationalise anthropomorphism (faces on content elements), it is the Numberblocks principle, and it avoids the seductive-details trap of an entertaining but irrelevant sidekick.
- **A recurring guide character is still defensible** for series identity and warmth (parasocial evidence), provided it *does* things in the explanation (points, reacts to the misconception, asks the viewer's question) rather than idling on screen.
- **Age-appropriateness (design judgement, not tested evidence):** Kurzgesagt shows that round shapes, simple faces and saturated colour need not read as babyish when (a) the palette is controlled (dark or muted backgrounds with a few saturated accents rather than primary-colour-everything), (b) faces are minimal (dots and a line, limited expressions), (c) the script's tone, humour and topics respect the viewer's age, and (d) there are no nursery cues (sing-song delivery, giggling sound effects, bouncy constant motion). For teens/adults with dyslexia the insult risk is in tone and content level more than in shape language.
- **Visual consistency across a series** is supported indirectly: familiarity drives the character benefit, and a fixed colour-meaning mapping acts as permanent signaling. It is also the main antidote to the AI look (section 5).
- Effect sizes of ~0.3 are worth having but smaller than the damage a non-explanatory visual does; emotional design is polish on top of sections 1–2, not a substitute.

### Gaps
- Plass et al. (2014) shape-vs-colour specifics (commonly summarised as round face-like shapes helping comprehension, warm colours helping affect) were not retrieved; do not cite those specifics without checking the paper.
- Age moderators from Wong & Adesope (2021) were not retrieved; I found no emotional-design or character study on adolescents with dyslexia.
- No research found on when a style is perceived as "babyish" by teen/adult struggling readers — an important practical question that should be answered by showing 2–3 style frames to a few target learners.
- Calvert et al. (2013) results were not retrieved in detail; the parasocial literature is overwhelmingly on toddlers/preschoolers.

## 5. What makes video read as "AI-made", how viewers react, and how to avoid it (incl. disclosure rules)

### Takeaway
Audience enthusiasm for AI-generated content fell sharply between 2023 and 2025, and viewers who suspect AI report lower trust and engagement — but learning outcomes from well-made AI video are generally on par with human-made video, and what people object to is low-effort sameness, robotic delivery and deception rather than AI assistance as such. The practical defence is human authorship where it shows (script with a point of view, storyboard where every shot explains, fixed style bible, deterministic animation, good audio, an editing pass); stylised animated explainers do not require YouTube's altered-content label, but generic template-like output is now explicitly a monetisation risk.

### Cited Findings

**Viewer attitudes**
- **[VENDOR/INDUSTRY]** eMarketer: consumer enthusiasm for AI-generated creator content dropped from 60% (2023) to 26% (2025). — [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/exclusive--ai-slop-threat-creator-economy)
- **[VENDOR]** Animoto 2026 State of Video report: ~82–83% of consumers believe they can identify AI-created video / have watched video they suspected was AI; 36% say AI video lowers their trust in a brand; top giveaway cited is robotic gestures (67%); over a third trust AI video as much as human-made. Sample size not found. — [Business Wire release](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260121875037/en) (page itself returned 403; figures from search summary and trade-press copies, e.g. [Adweek wire](https://www.adweek.com/adweek-wire/83-of-consumers-can-spot-ai-videos-36-say-it-hurts-brand-trust-new-animoto-report/))
- **[VENDOR aggregator; verify originals]** Kapwing's statistics round-up: 52% report reduced engagement with content they believe is AI-generated; 84–91% want AI labelling to be standard/required; commonly cited "tells" include content that sounds too formal or robotic (49%); disclosing AI up front causes mild scepticism but avoids the stronger backlash of feeling deceived afterwards (Journal of Advertising Research, 2025, three experiments). AI "slop" density on TikTok: 35.0% in Science & Education and 57.4% in the Kids category (Kapwing 2025 data); 21% of the first 500 YouTube Shorts served to a new account were AI-generated. — [Kapwing, 55 AI-generated video statistics](https://www.kapwing.com/resources/55-ai-generated-video-statistics-disclosure-detection-and-trust/)
- **[INDUSTRY]** YouTube was reported to be testing a post-view survey asking viewers whether a video "feels like AI slop". — [TVREV](https://www.tvrev.com/news/could-ai-undermine-youtubes-mission-to-grab-more-tv-ad-dollars) (search-summary level)

**AI-generated instructional video: learning vs liking**
- **[MODERATE]** A 2025 BJET study: AI-generated instructional videos facilitated learning as effectively as recorded videos (better retention for English word learning); no significant differences in satisfaction, motivation, trust, cognitive load, emotion or parasocial interaction, though the AI group's ratings were slightly lower. — [Paper PDF copy](https://www.tiffin.edu/wp-content/uploads/Article-From-Recording-to-AI-Instructional-Video.pdf) (search-summary level)
- **[MODERATE]** JMIR Formative Research (2026), AI avatars vs human presenters: user-experience ratings consistently higher for the human-presented videos; learning-outcome difference not significant. — [JMIR Formative Research](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e90037/PDF)
- **[MODERATE]** "The Reel Deal" (2025), 62 university students, LLM-generated short-form educational videos: mixed results; learners often prefer human content for relatability and engagement. — [arXiv 2509.05962](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05962v1)
- **[VENDOR]** 2026 descriptive survey of 170 computing students: comfort with AI video split about evenly, only about 1 in 10 preferred AI video to instructor-recorded video, fewer than 1 in 5 trusted it equally. — [Knowlify write-up](https://knowlify.com/articles/ai-generated-instructional-videos-student-study); [independent summary](https://welcome.ai/content/ai-generated-instructional-videos-student-reception-and-trust-issues)
- **[VENDOR]** TechSmith (2025), ~768 workers, four narration versions of a 45-second video: audio *quality* mattered more than human-vs-AI source; natural-sounding AI voices were often not distinguished from human; low-quality synthetic voices were "unmistakable and draw attention away from the content"; retention tracked voice quality. — [TechSmith](https://techsmith.com/blog/ai-voices-avatars-in-training-videos)

**The tells**
- **[PRACTITIONER/VENDOR]** Commonly listed visual/audio tells: skin that is too smooth or waxy; imperfect audio–video alignment and a disconnect between generated visuals and separately added audio; robotic gestures; formal/robotic-sounding language. Classic artefacts (six fingers, melting subjects) are fading with newer models. — [VEED detection guide](https://www.veed.io/learn/how-to-tell-if-video-is-ai-generated); [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/ai/ai-generated-videos-are-entering-a-truly-troubling-era); [Kapwing statistics](https://www.kapwing.com/resources/55-ai-generated-video-statistics-disclosure-detection-and-trust/)
- **[MODERATE]** Text rendered inside AI-generated video remains unreliable across models (gibberish or unreadable, especially with motion or perspective change). — [T2VTextBench (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04946v1)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** In AI "hand-drawn" illustration the give-aways are inconsistent line weight, wrong/absent paper texture, mixed sub-styles, drift once a set grows beyond roughly 30 assets, and fake hand-drawn made by filtering a clean vector; recommended counter-measures are a 2–4 colour palette with one dominant colour and 1–2 accents, real texture (paper grain, ink bleed), and locking line weight and palette roles in the prompt/reference. — [GetIllustrations 2026 trend report](https://getillustrations.com/blog/hand-drawn-illustration-ai-era-2026/) (vendor blog; opinion)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Example of a style rule set used as a generation constraint: bold black outlines, a three-colour palette, single-colour background, flat fills, no gradients. — [Canopy visual generative guidelines](https://www.getcanopy.com/wp-content/themes/get_canopy/assets/docs/canopy-visual-generative-guidelines.md)

**Platform rules (as of 2026)**
- **[PRIMARY]** YouTube altered/synthetic content disclosure applies to *realistic* content: a real person appearing to say/do something they did not, altered footage of real events/places, realistic scenes that never occurred. **Not required** for: clearly unrealistic content including "AI-animated objects in a fully animated video" and fantastical scenes; production assistance (AI for scripts, outlines, titles, thumbnails, infographics, idea generation); captions; cloning *your own* voice for voiceovers or dubs; minor aesthetic edits. Disclosure itself does not limit audience or monetisation eligibility; consistent non-disclosure where required can lead to a label being applied by YouTube, content removal or suspension from the Partner Programme. — [YouTube Help: Disclosing use of altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)
- **[INDUSTRY]** YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" monetisation policy "inauthentic content" on 15 July 2025. — [Logie summary](https://logie.ai/news/youtube-ai-slop-crackdown-2026-monetization/)
- **[INDUSTRY, reporting YouTube's wording]** 13 July 2026 clarification lists three kinds of inauthentic content that cannot be monetised: (1) generic or repetitive content that looks template-made across a channel's videos; (2) unsatisfying or off-putting content (emotionally manipulative formulas, interchangeable videos, stitched unrelated AI clips, deceptive realistic fakes); (3) AI personas presented as human experts advising on sensitive topics (health, legal, finance, politics). AI use in itself is allowed; described as clarification rather than new policy. — [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/07/13/youtube-inauthentic-content-monetization-policy-update/)
- **[SECONDARY]** TikTok requires visible labels on AI-generated visuals/audio depicting realistic people or scenes; EU AI Act transparency rules take effect August 2026. — [Kapwing statistics round-up](https://www.kapwing.com/resources/55-ai-generated-video-statistics-disclosure-detection-and-trust/) / [VEED guide](https://www.veed.io/learn/how-to-tell-if-video-is-ai-generated) (search-summary level; check primary texts, especially AI Act Article 50, for any obligation relevant to an Iceland/EEA creator)

### Inferences
- **What reads as "AI-made" in illustrated explainers (synthesis of the tells above plus the creator's own experience):**
  1. *Default aesthetics* — the model's house look (glossy gradients, soft 3D "clay" render, over-detailed lighting) or generic flat stock-icon look; nothing was chosen.
  2. *Inconsistency* — characters, line weight, palette or perspective change from shot to shot.
  3. *Motion without meaning* — morphing/warping, slow drifting "camera" over a still, things moving that have no reason to move.
  4. *Broken text* — garbled or pseudo-letters inside images (fatal for a literacy-related audience, and Icelandic characters þ, ð, æ, ö are a likely failure point for image models — untested here).
  5. *Narration* — flat prosody, wrong stress, mispronounced names, no breaths or pace changes.
  6. *Script* — generic phrasing, no opinion, no concrete example, no humour, no acknowledged difficulty.
  7. *Sync* — pictures loosely "about" the topic rather than changing exactly when the narrator names something.
  8. *Sameness* — every video on the channel feels interchangeable (now also a stated monetisation risk on YouTube).
- **Counter-measures, cheapest first (inference; each maps to a tell):**
  - **Style bible, written once:** 4–6 colour palette with fixed meanings, one line weight, one texture (paper/grain overlay applied in compositing so it is identical everywhere), one or two fonts, fixed character proportions, a short "never" list (no gradients, no photoreal, no text inside generated images). Apply texture and colour grading as a *deterministic post step* so even imperfect generations converge on one look.
  - **Character sheets and reference images:** generate each recurring character once (turnaround plus expressions), approve it, then always generate from those references; build a reusable asset library rather than re-generating per video. Assets used many times are consistent by construction.
  - **Human storyboard:** the human decides, for every sentence, what mechanism the shot shows (section 2 test). This is where "point of view" enters and it is the step AI templates skip.
  - **Deterministic motion:** animate still assets (cut-outs, SVG, layered illustrations) in code or a motion tool so nothing morphs; movement is only ever the mechanism or a cue. Add all text as real type in the compositor, never inside generated images.
  - **Narration first, animate to it:** time cues to word timestamps so highlights land on the spoken word (Kurzgesagt's voice-sets-timing practice).
  - **Audio:** the TechSmith result suggests voice quality matters more than whether it is AI; a real human voice (the creator's own, or a clone of their own voice — which YouTube exempts from disclosure) with natural pacing is the single strongest "a person made this" signal available, and matters doubly because the narration is the primary channel for this audience. Add purposeful sound effects on key events and a consistent music bed kept low.
  - **Human edit pass:** cut anything that does not explain; fix pronunciation; add one or two specific, local, human touches per video (an Icelandic everyday example, a joke, an admitted "this is the tricky part").
- **Hand-drawn / textured styles help twice:** they are the style with the best learning-and-enjoyment evidence (section 1) and they are furthest from the glossy default; imperfection reads as human. But *fake* hand-drawn with inconsistent line weight reads as AI immediately, so consistency controls still apply.
- **Disclosure:** a stylised, fully animated explainer with AI-assisted illustrations and script help falls under YouTube's listed *exemptions* (unrealistic/animated content; production assistance), so no altered-content label is required; a synthetic voice that is not a clone of a real other person is not listed as requiring disclosure either. Given that viewers punish *perceived deception* more than disclosed AI use, a short honest line in the description ("illustrations made with AI assistance, script and teaching by X") is a low-cost trust measure — my recommendation, not a platform requirement.
- **For this audience the trust issue is sharper:** parents, teachers and special-education staff choose the content; a channel that looks like the "AI slop" that is dense in kids/education feeds will be dismissed before the pedagogy is noticed.

### Gaps
- No rigorous study found on which specific features make *animated/illustrated* (as opposed to photoreal) content read as AI; the tell list is synthesis of practitioner sources plus inference.
- Animoto primary report (sample, method) not accessible; Kapwing figures are an aggregation with many unattributed years — treat all percentages as indicative.
- No data found on attitudes of parents/teachers or of learners with dyslexia toward AI-made educational video, and none for Iceland.
- EU AI Act transparency obligations (deployer duties for AI-generated content) were not checked at primary-source level; whether and how they apply to a solo creator in Iceland (EEA) is unverified.
- Quality of Icelandic text-to-speech / voice cloning in 2026 was out of scope here and not researched; it is decisive for the narration recommendation.
- YouTube policy on "made for kids" classification and any education-specific rules were not covered by the fetched page.

## 6. Current (2026) AI tooling for a solo creator making illustrated explainers

### Takeaway
As of 2026 the workable division of labour is: image models with multi-reference input for consistent illustrated assets; programmatic or keyframed motion graphics for anything that must be exact (diagrams, labels, step-by-step builds, timing to narration); generative video only for short, non-critical atmospheric shots, if at all. Tool names and versions change within months — the pattern is more durable than the products.

### Cited Findings
- **[PRACTITIONER, fast-changing]** Image-model providers converged in 2026 on the same consistency mechanism: supply one or more reference images along with the prompt. Reported limits: Google's "Nano Banana Pro" up to 14 reference images; OpenAI's gpt-image-2 up to 16; Flux 2 Pro up to 8 via API; Midjourney V7 Omni Reference takes one image, with a multi-reference Edit Model (up to four) for V8.x. Version naming conflicts between sources. — [Apiframe developer guide](https://apiframe.ai/guides/character-consistency-ai-image-api); [TechStackups comparison, May 2026](https://techstackups.com/comparisons/gemini-vs-openai-vs-flux-vs-runway-character-consistency-may-2026/); [Kive guide](https://kive.ai/learn/same-model-across-ai-product-photos)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** "No generative route guarantees identical identity, outfit and proportions in every scene" — reference images reduce but do not eliminate drift; independent head-to-head testing is scarce and mostly vendor-written. — [Kive guide](https://kive.ai/learn/same-model-across-ai-product-photos); [anecdotal benchmark](https://rentierdigital.xyz/blog/ai-image-model-character-consistency-benchmark)
- **[MODERATE]** Text-to-video / image-to-video models (Veo, Kling, Sora and peers) still fail at rendering on-screen text reliably; text under motion or perspective change is especially poor; ability to render precise captions or mathematical formulas "remains largely untested"; models are strongest on short clips (roughly 5–20 seconds). — [T2VTextBench (arXiv 2505.04946)](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04946v1); [2026 model comparison](https://diyai.io/ai-tools/video-generation/ai-video-generation-models/) (blog-level)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** Programmatic video tools: **Remotion** (React components; source-available, company licence required above a threshold; renders through a headless browser; favoured for automation and data-driven batch video); **Motion Canvas** (TypeScript, generator-based timelines, MIT licence, built for "informative vector animation", editor with voiceover sync; often chosen for explainers); **Manim** (Python; maths/science visuals; community edition actively maintained; scenes typically hand-crafted). All three are described as "agent-friendly" because scenes are structured code, but the creator must supply the validation loop. — [Remotion's own comparison with Motion Canvas](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/compare/motion-canvas) (not neutral); [comparison article](https://beginnersinai.org/remotion-vs-motion-canvas-vs-manim/); [PkgPulse guide](https://www.pkgpulse.com/guides/remotion-vs-motion-canvas-vs-revideo-programmatic-video-2026)
- **[PRACTITIONER]** 3Blue1Brown is the existence proof that a (near-)solo creator can run a celebrated explainer channel on fully programmatic animation. — [3Blue1Brown FAQ](https://www.3blue1brown.com/faq/)

### Inferences
- **Why programmatic motion is more accurate for diagrams:** a generative video model re-imagines every frame, so shapes, counts, labels and proportions can drift and nothing can be pinned to a narration timestamp; code-based animation is deterministic (same input, same frames), positions and timings are explicit, text is real type (correct Icelandic characters guaranteed), and components — arrow, highlight, draw-on line, character with expressions, fraction bar — are reusable across every video, which is also what creates series consistency. This follows from the tool descriptions and the text-rendering benchmark; I found no study measuring it directly.
- **Suggested solo pipeline (inference):** script → per-sentence storyboard (human) → narration recorded/generated, word timestamps extracted → still assets generated with a multi-reference image model against the style bible and character sheets (transparent-background cut-outs or vectorised to SVG) → scenes assembled in Remotion / Motion Canvas (LLM-written code, human-reviewed renders) with draw-on reveals, highlights and simple transforms → shared texture/grade overlay → sound design → human edit pass.
- **Where generative video might still earn a place:** an occasional short establishing or mood shot with no text and no mechanism, heavily style-matched; it should never carry the explanation. Each such shot is also where "AI look" risk is highest.
- **Rive/Lottie/After Effects** (named in the brief) fit the same deterministic role for character rigs and reusable micro-animations; I found no sourced comparison in this session.

### Gaps
- All tooling claims come from vendor or SEO-style comparison pages; version names conflict (e.g. "Nano Banana 2" vs "Nano Banana Pro", "gpt-image-2" vs "GPT Image 2.5"). Verify against official documentation at build time.
- No independent benchmark found of how reliably current LLMs write correct Remotion / Motion Canvas / Manim scenes, nor of render-and-check loops.
- No evidence gathered on image-model handling of Icelandic text in images (assumed unreliable; avoid in-image text regardless).
- Rive, Lottie and After Effects were not researched.
- Cost per video for any of these pipelines was not researched.

## 7. Screen layout for low readers (dyslexia, co-occurring ADHD, visual stress)

### Takeaway
Let narration and pictures carry the content; keep on-screen text to a few large key terms shown exactly when they are spoken, in a well-spaced sans-serif on a non-glaring background, with uncluttered frames. The research on narration vs on-screen text for dyslexic learners is genuinely mixed (children benefit from audio; some adult studies favour text), so the defensible position is audio-led with optional, well-designed text support (key terms, user-controllable captions) rather than no text at all.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** Animation benefits are largest when there is *no accompanying text* (g = 0.883) and with auditory commentary (g = 0.336). — [Berney & Bétrancourt 2016](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:92234)
- **[MODERATE]** Children with dyslexia (age 11; 26 dyslexic, 38 typical readers; Knoop-van Campen, Segers & Verhoeven 2018): modality and reversed-redundancy effects for *study time* — audio or audio support was more efficient for children with dyslexia; no modality/redundancy effect on knowledge gain. — [Univ. of Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/the-modality-and-redundancy-effects-in-multimedia-learning-in-chi/); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6084336)
- **[MODERATE, counter-evidence]** College students: Dawson et al. (2021, Annals of Dyslexia; 148 participants, 2×2×2 design) found a *reverse* modality effect for students with dyslexia (better with on-screen text than narration), while pictures improved performance for all groups. A related study with 73 college students with dyslexia also found on-screen text gave better recall/recognition than spoken text, and pictures aided recognition. — [Annals of Dyslexia 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-021-00219-z); [J. Special Education Technology study](https://doi.org/10.1177/0162643418754530) (abstract/secondary)
- **[STRONG]** Pictures help across groups in those studies; signaling helps most for low-prior-knowledge learners. — [Annals of Dyslexia 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11881-021-00219-z); [Richter et al. 2016 meta-analysis](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/signaling-text-picture-relations-in-multimedia-learning-a-comprehensive-meta-analysis/814569585163370497-11949)
- **[GUIDANCE]** Dyslexia Scotland: letter, word and line spacing matter more than typeface; WCAG minimum contrast 4.5:1, but people with visual stress often prefer lower text-to-background contrast and preferences vary individually; "where possible, provide video-based alternatives to typed formats". — [Dyslexia Scotland, dyslexia-friendly formats](https://dyslexiascotland.org.uk/dyslexia-friendly-typed-formats)
- **[GUIDANCE, secondhand]** British Dyslexia Association style guide (2018): larger inter-letter spacing (around 35% of average letter width); avoid pure black text on pure white backgrounds. — [Smashing Magazine summary of the BDA guide](https://smashingmagazine.com/2021/11/dyslexia-friendly-mode-website)
- **[GUIDANCE]** Use sans-serif fonts for on-screen material; left-align, do not fully justify; prefer fonts with open letter shapes and clear gaps. — [Open University OpenLearn](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/education-development/education-careers/accessibility-elearning/content-section-3.4); [Ulster University accessibility guidance](https://www.ulster.ac.uk/accessibility-standards/creating-and-improving-accessible-content); [TetraLogical, designing for reading disabilities (2026)](https://tetralogical.com/blog/2026/06/25/designing-for-reading-disabilities/)
- **[STRONG]** Irrelevant, constantly present visual additions reduce learning; animations are often too fast or complex to be perceived. — [Seductive details summary](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [Tversky et al. 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)
- **[STRONG for engagement]** Segment into short chunks (under 6 minutes). — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Layout rules that are cheap to enforce as template defaults (inference from the above):**
  - Maximum one short label (1–3 words) on screen at a time; it appears when the narrator says it and is placed next to the thing it names (acts as signaling, not as reading load). No sentences, no bullet lists, no text-dense slides.
  - Label type: large sans-serif with generous letter spacing, sentence case, dark text on an off-white/cream or soft-tinted panel or light text on a dark muted background; avoid pure black on pure white; keep contrast at or above 4.5:1 but not harsh.
  - Because the Icelandic key term is itself often the learning target (spelling and recognition), showing the *one* key word while it is spoken is justified; the adult-dyslexia studies favouring text argue against banning text entirely.
  - Background: flat or lightly textured, static; no patterned, striped, flashing or constantly animated backgrounds (visual stress, ADHD distraction; seductive-details evidence). Texture grain should be subtle and not shimmer frame to frame.
  - One focal point per shot; everything not being discussed is dimmed or absent; generous empty space; consistent positions for recurring elements (title term always in the same place).
  - Motion: slow enough to follow, only on the element being explained; hold the completed picture for a beat before moving on (apprehension principle); no rapid cuts.
  - Chunking: short segments with a clear visual "chapter" marker; recap frame that re-shows the built diagram rather than a text summary.
  - Captions: offer as user-toggleable subtitles rather than burned-in word-by-word kinetic captions, so the frame stays clean for those who find moving text effortful while those who benefit from text can turn it on.
- The same configuration that the animation meta-analyses favour for everyone (narrated, low-text, representational, cued) is the dyslexia-friendly one; there is no trade-off between "accessible" and "effective" here.
