# Designing explainer videos for autistic (and autistic + dyslexic) 14–16-year-olds, at a teenage tone

Scope note: researched November 2026. Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[STRONG]** = peer-reviewed meta-analysis / systematic review or formal standard
- **[MODERATE]** = individual peer-reviewed studies, small samples, or lab tasks not tested on explainer videos
- **[PRACTICE]** = practitioner / autism-organisation consensus, not experimentally tested on video
- **[INFERENCE]** = my reasoning from the above, applied to this video format
- Items marked *(not re-fetched this session)* come from well-known standards / papers whose URLs and content I am confident of but did not open during this session; the report writer may want to spot-check them.

Important framing finding: **there is almost no direct research on narrated, animated school-subject explainer videos for autistic teenagers.** Most of the concrete do's and don'ts below are transferred from (a) video-modelling research (mostly younger children, social/functional skills), (b) general multimedia-learning research (non-autistic samples), (c) language/pragmatics lab studies, and (d) practitioner guidance. Say this plainly in the report.

## 1. How do autistic learners learn from video/multimedia? What helps comprehension and what causes overload?

### Takeaway
Video is a well-established teaching medium for autistic learners (video modelling is an evidence-based practice), but the evidence is mostly about social, functional and job skills, not academic content, and mostly with younger children. The design principles most consistently recommended for autistic multimedia learners are the same "cognitive load" principles from general multimedia research, applied more strictly: remove extraneous stimuli, segment content, and keep narration and visuals tightly synchronised.

### Cited Findings
- A systematic review of 39 video-modelling studies found the most-targeted skills were social skills (23 studies) and functional/employment skills (11), with **academic skills and "other" in only 5 studies** — academic use of video modelling is under-researched. **[STRONG for the gap; reported via search summary]** — [PMC8992915](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8992915)
- A systematic review of video self-modelling for students aged 3–17 concluded its effect on **academic** skills "has only been examined in a limited number of studies" and more research is needed. **[STRONG for the gap]** — [Springer, Journal of Behavioral Education](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10864-013-9189-x)
- A 2021 meta-analysis concluded video modelling is an overall effective intervention for teaching **job skills to autistic adolescents and adults** — i.e., video works for this age group, but for procedural skills. **[STRONG, but different content type]** — [pubmed 36601641](https://www.pubmed.gov/36601641/)
- A meta-analysis of single-case studies with autistic adolescents and adults found medium-to-strong effects for behavioural interventions across academic, adaptive, social and vocational skills (video not isolated). **[STRONG, indirect]** — [informahealthcare 10.1080/20473869.2022.2039452](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/ref/10.1080/20473869.2022.2039452)
- A 2018 meta-analysis of tablet-mediated instruction covered 19 studies targeting academic skills for autistic individuals. **[STRONG, indirect]** — [BSL](https://mijn.bsl.nl/meta-analysis-of-tablet-mediated-interventions-for-teaching-acad/15656432)
- Video modelling, visual supports, prompting and time delay are all listed among evidence-based practices for autistic learners in the US NCAEP review (Steinbrenner et al., 2020). **[STRONG]** — [NCAEP / FPG Child Development Institute](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/research-resources/) *(not re-fetched this session)*
- Bellini & Akullian's (2007) meta-analysis found video modelling and video self-modelling effective for autistic children/adolescents, with skills maintained and generalised. **[STRONG]** — [Exceptional Children 73(3)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001440290707300301) *(not re-fetched this session)*
- A paper revisiting multimedia design principles for autistic learners (in VR) highlights **minimising external stimuli, segmenting content, and synchronising audio-visual presentation** as principles thought to lower cognitive load, and notes autistic learners "may be more sensitive to sensory stimuli." **[MODERATE / review-level argument]** — [ResearchGate: Revisiting multimedia learning design principles in VR for autistic individuals](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373257449_Revisiting_multimedia_learning_design_principles_in_virtual_reality-based_learning_environments_for_autistic_individuals)
- A digital-storytelling study reported that combined verbal + audio-visual material assisted autistic children's learning, and deliberately left out unmentioned stimuli (e.g., unnecessary video) "as avoidance of excessive stimuli." It also built the content around the children's **circumscribed (special) interests**. **[MODERATE, small study]** — [ResearchGate: Digital storytelling & circumscribed interests](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369874357_Using_Digital_Storytelling_to_Improve_Knowledge_Acquisition_of_Circumscribed_Interests_of_Children_with_Autism_Spectrum_Disorder)
- General multimedia-learning principles (Mayer): coherence (cut extraneous material), signalling (cue structure), segmenting (learner-paced chunks), modality (words as narration rather than on-screen text), redundancy (don't duplicate narration with full on-screen text), temporal contiguity (words and pictures at the same time). **[STRONG for general population; not specifically tested with autistic teens]** — [Mayer, Multimedia Learning, Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/multimedia-learning/FB7E79A165D24D47CEACEB4D2C426ECD) *(not re-fetched this session)*
- Autistic reading profiles: reading difficulties associated with autism are often **comprehension/inference** difficulties rather than decoding (dyslexia-type) difficulties; many autistic students read words well but struggle with inference. **[PRACTICE / state education guidance]** — [Colorado Department of Education](https://www.cde.state.co.us/node/43723); [AdLit](https://www.adlit.org/autism-spectrum-disorder-asd-and-adolescents)
- Special interests: building material around autistic learners' interests is used in research as an engagement lever (see digital-storytelling study above). **[MODERATE]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369874357_Using_Digital_Storytelling_to_Improve_Knowledge_Acquisition_of_Circumscribed_Interests_of_Children_with_Autism_Spectrum_Disorder)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE]** The creator's existing dyslexia rules (narration carries content, short keyword labels, one idea per sentence, no music under explanation) already match the multimedia principles that the autism literature leans on (modality, redundancy, coherence). The autism-specific additions are mostly about *predictability*, *literal language*, *explicit inference*, and *sensory restraint*.
- **[INFERENCE]** Because autistic comprehension difficulties are often about inference, the narration should **state the "so what" explicitly** (e.g., "This matters because…", "So the result is…") rather than leaving the viewer to infer the link between two facts. This is "simple language, not simple ideas": keep the concept at grade level, but make every logical step audible.
- **[INFERENCE]** Detail-focused attention means unexplained visual details may be studied instead of the main point. Every drawn element should either be referenced by the narration or be removed. Background decoration with "interesting" details is a distraction risk.
- **[INFERENCE]** Examples drawn from common teen special-interest areas (games, trains/transport, animals, space, coding, history facts) can be used as worked examples, but should be concrete and not "cool-kid" slang.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing explainer-video formats (e.g., animation style, narration speed, pause length) specifically with autistic 14–16-year-olds.
- No study found on autistic learners and **academic** explainer videos (as opposed to video modelling of behaviours).
- I did not find direct research on processing speed in autism applied to narration rate (words per minute).

## 2. Language: literal vs figurative, explicit instructions, signposting, "answer in your head" prompts, and conversational tone

### Takeaway
Autistic adolescents, on average, find irony/sarcasm harder than peers, and autistic and dyslexic children both score below peers on figurative language — so narration should avoid unexplained idioms, sarcasm, vague hedges and rhetorical questions that aren't real questions. But the research does **not** support stripping language to a robotic literal register: figurative competence is teachable, and much of the difficulty is tied to grammar/language level. Resolution: conversational *voice and warmth*, literal *content and instructions*.

### Cited Findings
- 2025 fMRI study: autistic adolescents showed lower irony comprehension than age-matched typical controls. **[MODERATE]** — [Cerebral Cortex 35(2) bhaf003](https://dbpia.nl.go.kr/cercor/article/35/2/bhaf003/8006048)
- Irony understanding correlates with theory-of-mind / understanding of social situations; a 2024 follow-up found ToM uniquely contributes to **irony** (but not **idiom**) understanding. **[MODERATE]** — [Bar-Ilan University](https://education.biu.ac.il/en/node/11968); [Bar-Ilan](https://education.biu.ac.il/en/node/12112)
- When face-to-face cues are removed (computer-mediated dialogue), children with HFA/AS performed as well as typical peers on verbal irony measures — **presentation format matters**. **[MODERATE]** — [PMC3991690](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3991690)
- Autistic adults: intact comprehension of indirect requests in context, but marked difficulties with irony. **[MODERATE]** — [Springer 10.1007/s10803-018-3561-6](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10803-018-3561-6)
- Adolescents with ASD processed semantic jokes as fast as matched peers but less accurately (preprint). **[MODERATE, not peer reviewed]** — [preprints.org 202304.0793](https://preprints.org/manuscript/202304.0793)
- 2026 study (Kritsotakis et al., *Research in Developmental Disabilities*): **autistic and dyslexic children both scored well below peers on figurative tasks**; grammar skill (not age or reading level) was the strongest predictor of figurative understanding; recommends introducing figurative expressions with simple sentence structures. **[MODERATE, read via summary only]** — [MDPI Behavioral Sciences 15(11):1556 / related](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328x/15/11/1556)
- A systematic review (7 studies) found figurative-language skills in autism respond to intervention; effective programmes combined explicit teaching, visual supports, multiple examples and planned generalisation. **[MODERATE–STRONG, small review]** — [MDPI Behavioral Sciences](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/11/1556)
- A New Zealand thesis found high-school teachers were surprised how much non-literal language they used with students with Asperger syndrome. **[MODERATE]** — [Victoria University of Wellington](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Do_all_students_understand_the_same_message_High_school_teachers_use_of_non-literal_expressions/17064341)
- Counter-argument: when matched on core language ability, autistic learners understand non-literal language almost as well as literal; a rigid literal-only approach risks limiting language exposure. **[PRACTICE / commentary]** — [Dyslexia Action](https://dyslexiaaction.org.uk/?p=15322)
- Practitioner guidance: avoid idioms/sarcasm unless explained; vague/open instructions ("write a bit about…") leave length, format and scope unclear for autistic students. **[PRACTICE]** — [Simply Psychology: Why do autistic people need clarity?](https://www.simplypsychology.org/autism-and-needing-clarity.html); [Twinkl: Say what you mean](https://www.twinkl.com/resource/t-s-3759-autism-awareness-literal-instructions-teacher-activity); [Bloomsbury: "Don't take it literally"](https://res.cloudinary.com/bloomsbury-publishing-public/raw/upload/v1623315823/BloomsburyOnlineResources/BOR%20Children%27s%20Education/100%20Ideas%20for%20Primary%20Teachers:%20Supporting%20Pupils%20with%20Autism/Idea_19.docx)
- UK local-authority guidance: give processing time before repeating; chunk instructions so they are given and processed one step at a time. **[PRACTICE]** — [Devon County Council / Babcock](https://www.devon.gov.uk/support-schools-settings/?p=9086)
- Reading Rockets recommends teaching figurative language explicitly (idioms, metaphors, similes) with context clues. **[PRACTICE]** — [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/helping-all-readers/neurodiversity-and-children-learning-differences/children-autism-spectrum)

### Inferences — concrete script rules
- **[INFERENCE] Do:** Keep a warm, natural, first-/second-person voice ("Við skulum skoða…", "Þú sérð hér…"). Conversational tone ≠ figurative language. Warmth comes from voice, pacing and direct address, not from idioms or irony.
- **[INFERENCE] Don't:** sarcasm or ironic asides ("Frábært, akkúrat það sem við vildum…" when meaning the opposite); joke-by-understatement; Icelandic idioms used as content carriers (e.g., "að slá tvær flugur í einu höggi", "þetta er ekki flókið mál" when it is). If a subject *requires* an idiom or metaphor (e.g., literature/Icelandic class), name it as one: "Þetta er orðatiltæki. Það þýðir…".
- **[INFERENCE] Don't:** vague hedges and filler ("svona", "eiginlega", "nokkurn veginn", "kind of", "a bit", "some stuff"). They make claims fuzzy. If a quantity is uncertain, say exactly how ("um það bil 70%", "oftast, en ekki alltaf").
- **[INFERENCE] Rhetorical questions:** a question the narrator answers immediately ("Og hvað gerist þá? Jú…") is ambiguous — is it the viewer's turn or not? Reserve question intonation for **real** viewer questions only, and mark them with the same verbal and visual cue every time (see section 4). Replace rhetorical questions with statements: "Nú skoðum við hvað gerist næst."
- **[INFERENCE] Consistent terminology:** pick one term per concept and never swap synonyms for style (e.g., don't alternate "frumeind / atóm / ögn"). Literal-minded viewers may assume a new word = a new thing. If two terms are both used in the curriculum, say so once: "Frumeind er líka kölluð atóm. Í þessu myndbandi notum við orðið frumeind."
- **[INFERENCE] Signposting:** start with an explicit list of what is coming ("Í þessu myndbandi lærir þú þrennt: 1… 2… 3…"), number sections aloud, and end with the same list as a recap. This matches the predictability/"structured teaching" principle (section 6) and Mayer's signalling principle.
- **[INFERENCE] Pronouns and references:** avoid "this", "that", "it" across sentences when the referent could be ambiguous ("Það breytist" → "Hitastigið breytist"). Repeat the noun. This also fits the dyslexia "one idea per sentence" rule.
- **[INFERENCE] Make inferences explicit:** add the linking sentence ("Þess vegna…", "Það þýðir að…") instead of leaving the viewer to connect cause and effect.
- **[INFERENCE] "Answer in your head":** "think about it" / "hugsaðu um það" is an ambiguous instruction (think about what, for how long, then what?). Replace with a concrete action: "Segðu svarið upphátt", "Skrifaðu eitt orð á blaðið", "Veldu A eða B". Say how long you get and what happens next.
- **[INFERENCE] Where conversation and clarity conflict:** the main conflict points are (1) humour that relies on irony, (2) rhetorical questions, (3) hedging filler, (4) synonym variation for "nicer" style, (5) figures of speech. Keep everything else conversational: contractions, "við", direct address, real-life examples, a calm friendly voice.

- I could not read full texts of the 2025/2026 figurative-language papers; effect sizes not verified.

## 3. Sensory design: sound, motion, flashing, colour, clutter, pacing

### Takeaway
Hard rules exist only for flashing (WCAG 2.3.1, Ofcom broadcast guidance) and auto-moving content (WCAG 2.2.2); everything else for autism (sudden noises, music, volume jumps, busy backgrounds, shaking/bouncing elements) is practitioner consensus plus the general "minimise extraneous stimuli" principle. Sensory sensitivity is common in autism but highly individual, so the safest default is calm, consistent, low-surprise audio and motion.

### Cited Findings
- Multimedia design for autistic learners: minimise external stimuli; autistic learners may be more sensitive to sensory stimuli. **[MODERATE]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373257449_Revisiting_multimedia_learning_design_principles_in_virtual_reality-based_learning_environments_for_autistic_individuals)
- Practitioner/UDL guidance flags auto-playing video and flashing animations as barriers, and recommends adjustable audio levels, toggleable animation and interactive transcripts for sound-sensitive students. **[PRACTICE]** — [3Play Media: Video accessibility and UDL](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/video-accessibility-and-udl/); [Stratbeans](https://stratbeans.com/neurodiversity-in-elearning/); [Fullmind Learning](https://www.fullmindlearning.com/blog/online-learning-autism)
- **WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.1 Three Flashes or Below Threshold (Level A):** nothing flashes more than three times in any one-second period, or flashes stay below general and red flash thresholds. **[STRONG / standard]** — [W3C Understanding 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html) *(not re-fetched this session)*
- **WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (Level A):** moving, blinking or scrolling content that starts automatically, lasts more than 5 seconds and is shown alongside other content needs a way to pause/stop/hide it. For a video, the player's pause control covers the video as a whole; the principle for *inside* the video is to avoid decorative perpetual motion next to content. **[STRONG / standard]** — [W3C Understanding 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html) *(not re-fetched this session)*
- **WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA)** and **1.4.2 Audio Control** reflect the same concern about non-essential motion and unexpected sound. **[STRONG / standard]** — [W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) *(not re-fetched this session)*
- **Ofcom** Guidance Note on flashing images and regular patterns (Broadcasting Code rule 2.12) — UK broadcasters limit flashes to no more than three per second and avoid high-contrast regular patterns (stripes) filling the screen; content is checked with automated tools (e.g., Harding test). **[STRONG / regulator standard]** — [Ofcom: Guidance on flashing images and regular patterns](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/tv-radio-and-on-demand/broadcast-codes/broadcast-code) *(not re-fetched this session)*

### Inferences — concrete sensory rules
- **[INFERENCE] Sound — do:** one consistent, calm narrator voice; consistent volume across episodes (normalise loudness, e.g., to a single LUFS target); if a sound cue is used for "your turn", make it **the same soft, short, predictable sound every time**, and introduce it in episode 1 ("Þegar þú heyrir þetta hljóð er komið að þér").
- **[INFERENCE] Sound — don't:** sudden loud effects (whooshes, pops, buzzers, "wrong answer" honks), volume jumps between intro and content, high-pitched chimes, laughter tracks, music under explanation (already in the dyslexia rules). Short, quiet intro/outro music is acceptable if it is identical each episode and clearly separated from teaching.
- **[INFERENCE] Motion — do:** slow, purposeful motion that shows the concept (an arrow that grows, an object that moves from A to B); hold still frames while explaining; transitions as simple cuts or short fades, same style every time.
- **[INFERENCE] Motion — don't:** idle "bouncing", "wobbling", "breathing" or shaking elements; camera shake; fast zooms; whip-pans; rapid cuts; flashing or strobing emphasis (also a WCAG/Ofcom issue); high-contrast stripes/checkerboards filling the frame.
- **[INFERENCE] Visual density:** one focal object at a time; plain or very low-detail background; the keyword label (from the dyslexia guidance) placed in the same screen position every time; remove anything the narration does not mention (detail-focused viewers will study it).
- **[INFERENCE] Colour:** moderate saturation, high text/background contrast for labels (WCAG 1.4.3 ≥ 4.5:1 for text), consistent colour coding across the series (e.g., one colour always = definition, one always = example). Avoid neon/saturated full-screen colour flashes.
- **[INFERENCE] Pacing:** steady, unhurried narration with short breaths between ideas; don't speed up for "energy". Pacing is where autism, dyslexia and ADHD needs can conflict (see section 5) — resolve with short total length and segmenting rather than fast delivery.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve BBC accessibility/editorial guidance or National Autistic Society / Autism Education Trust sensory pages during this session; no quotes from them.
- No research found giving a numeric "safe" narration speed, sound-effect level or on-screen element count for autistic viewers.
- Individual variability (hypo- vs hyper-sensitivity) means no single setting suits all; flag in the report.

## 4. Questions and pauses in video: "your turn" prompts, phrasing, timing, recurring format

### Takeaway
Structured prompts with a short fixed wait are evidence-based practice for autistic learners ("time delay"), and predictable routines are a core autism-education principle. For video, a "your turn" moment should be announced the same way every time, state exactly what to do and how long there is, and resolve with the answer — with no social pressure ("everyone knows this!").

### Cited Findings
- **Time delay** is an evidence-based practice for autistic learners: present the cue, wait a fixed 3–5 seconds for the learner to respond, then prompt; in progressive time delay the wait grows (e.g., in 1-second steps) as mastery increases. **[STRONG as EBP; numbers are practice guidance]** — [Texas Education Agency SPED Support: Time Delay](https://spedsupport.tea.texas.gov/learning-library/evidence-based-practices-students-autism/time-delay); [Texas Autism Network: Time Delay steps](https://txautism.net/interventions/time-delay)
- A small study (2 autistic students, one-to-one) found brief wait time increased opportunities to respond and correct responses per minute, contradicting earlier work favouring longer waits for learners with intellectual/developmental disabilities. **[MODERATE, n=2]** — [NYU IFP summary (Lamella & Tincani)](https://ifp.nyu.edu/?p=326977)
- Practice guidance: allow processing time before repeating an instruction; chunk instructions one step at a time. **[PRACTICE]** — [Devon County Council / Babcock](https://www.devon.gov.uk/support-schools-settings/?p=9086)
- Structured teaching (TEACCH) and its components (e.g., visual schedules) are widely adopted internationally; the Autism CRC review rated evidence for structured teaching as inconsistent. **[MIXED]** — [Autism CRC: TEACCH interventions](https://www.autismcrc.com.au/node/64441); [ASAT](https://asatonline.org/?p=279)

### Inferences — concrete prompt design
- **[INFERENCE] Same format every time:** (1) fixed verbal cue ("Nú er komið að þér."), (2) the same visual frame (e.g., a question card in a fixed corner/colour), (3) the same soft sound if any, (4) a visible countdown bar or timer showing the length of the pause, (5) the answer, said aloud and shown, (6) a neutral confirmation ("Svarið er …").
- **[INFERENCE] Phrase the expected action exactly:** "Segðu svarið upphátt" / "Bentu á mynd A eða B" / "Skrifaðu eitt orð". Avoid "pældu í þessu" or "hvað heldur þú?" with no response mode.
- **[INFERENCE] Tell them the pause length and that they can pause the video:** "Þú færð fimm sekúndur. Þú mátt líka stoppa myndbandið." This respects variable processing time without making the default pause too long for ADHD viewers.
- **[INFERENCE] Pause length:** the 3–5 s time-delay figure is from live instruction of simple responses; a retrieval question about lesson content probably needs longer (~5–8 s) plus the "pause the video" option. This is not research-derived — test with real students.
- **[INFERENCE] No social pressure or judgement:** avoid "auðvitað vissir þú þetta", "þetta er auðvelt", "flestir klikka á þessu". After the answer, avoid both shaming and gushing praise; a neutral "Ef þú sagðir X, þá er það rétt. Ef ekki, er það í lagi — við förum aftur yfir það." is enough.
- **[INFERENCE] One question = one answer:** avoid open or multiple-correct-answer questions in the pause slots unless the answer explains that several answers are acceptable ("Það eru tvö rétt svör: …").
- **[INFERENCE] Recurring episode skeleton** (predictability across a series): same intro (short, identical), "Í dag lærir þú þrennt" agenda, numbered sections, the same "your turn" format, the same recap/outro. Change only the content, not the format.

### Gaps
- No study found testing "your turn" prompts or pause lengths *inside pre-recorded video* with autistic teenagers.
- Did not find NAS/AET wording specific to questioning; their sites were not successfully retrieved in this session.

## 5. Humour, characters/faces, co-occurrence (autism–dyslexia–ADHD), and not being babyish

### Takeaway
Irony and sarcasm are the riskiest forms of humour; gentle, literal, situation-based humour is lower-risk but there is no research showing it improves learning for autistic teens. Eye-tracking studies show autistic viewers look less at faces and social interactions in cartoons, so faces/emotions should not carry content. Co-occurrence is high (ADHD in ~28% of autistic people; dyslexia rates in autism reported 6–30%), so one video will often serve a viewer with two or three profiles. Teenagers reject content that feels immature or trivialising; the safest "teen" tone is respectful and matter-of-fact rather than jokey.

### Cited Findings
**Humour**
- Autistic adolescents show lower irony comprehension than peers. **[MODERATE]** — [Cerebral Cortex 2025](https://dbpia.nl.go.kr/cercor/article/35/2/bhaf003/8006048)
- Autistic adolescents process semantic jokes as fast but less accurately than matched peers. **[MODERATE, preprint]** — [preprints.org](https://preprints.org/manuscript/202304.0793)
- Teenagers in a 2017 qualitative study (n=16) said they prefer relaxed or comic approaches in educational video, short duration, fast editing and infographics. **[MODERATE, small, non-autistic sample]** — [IATED Digital Library (Almeida 2017)](https://library.iated.org/view/ALMEIDA2017PRE)

**Faces and characters**
- Geneva longitudinal eye-tracking (eLife 2024): autistic children's gaze on a popular cartoon diverged most from peers during **scenes of social interaction between characters**, and more so in children with lower developmental levels; autistic children develop their own idiosyncratic attention preferences. **[MODERATE–STRONG, young children]** — [eLife 85623](https://elifesciences.org/articles/85623); [UNIGE Neurocenter summary](https://neurocenter-unige.ch/how-does-social-attention-develop-in-autistic-children/)
- Riby et al. (2009): autistic children looked at faces less than typical peers in both live-action and cartoon clips; motion brought out the difference more than still images. **[MODERATE]** — [Behaviorist Book Club summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/looking-at-movies-and-cartoons--eye-tracking-evidence-from-williams-sy/)

**Co-occurrence**
- Lai et al. 2019 (*Lancet Psychiatry*, 96 studies): pooled prevalence in autistic people — **ADHD 28%**, anxiety disorders 20%, sleep–wake disorders 13%. **[STRONG]** — [PubMed 31447415](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31447415/); [UCL Discovery full text](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10081954/7/Mandy%20THELANCETPSYCH-D-19-00008R4_.pdf)
- Dyslexia among autistic children is reported between about **6% and 30%**, depending on definition (lower when defined narrowly as phonological disorder); many autism-related reading problems are comprehension/inference problems rather than dyslexia. **[PRACTICE summary of limited studies]** — [Colorado Department of Education](https://www.cde.state.co.us/node/43723)
- ADHD occurs in ~25–40% of children with reading disability vs ~5% generally; overlap partly attributed to shared genetic/neuropsychological factors. **[STRONG-ish, review]** — [PubMed 21757683](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21757683/)
- Neurodevelopmental problems co-occur commonly in dyslexia (cohort study). **[MODERATE–STRONG]** — [PubMed 33759268](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33759268/)
- Children sharing symptoms of autism, dyslexia and ADHD are discussed as a co-occurrence group. **[MODERATE, book chapter]** — [InTech chapter (Univ. Milan)](https://air.unimi.it/retrieve/dfa8b990-b4df-748b-e053-3a05fe0a3a96/InTech-Co_occurrence_of_developmental_disorders_children_who_share_symptoms_of_autism_dyslexia_and_attention_deficit_hyperactivity_disorder.pdf)

**Patronising / babyish content**

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] Humour — do:** at most light, literal, observational humour that is not needed for understanding (e.g., a deadpan factual absurdity that is clearly marked as a joke), placed *between* teaching points, never inside a definition or a question. **Don't:** sarcasm, irony, "jokes" where the narrator says the opposite of what's meant, mocking wrong answers, teasing the viewer.
- **[INFERENCE] Characters:** a narrator-host character is fine, but don't put meaning in facial expressions or character interactions ("look how confused Siggi is"). If emotion matters, say it ("Siggi er ruglaður af því að…"). Prefer simple, adult-proportioned or neutral designs over big-eyed, rounded "kids' TV" styling; avoid mascots that react with exaggerated emotions.
- **[INFERENCE] The pacing conflict:** ADHD viewers want brevity and variety; autistic viewers want predictability and calm; dyslexic viewers need time and narration. Resolve by: **short total length** (segments of a few minutes), **visual change that is content-driven** (a new diagram per idea, not decorative motion), **steady but not slow narration**, and **interaction via the "your turn" slots** rather than fast cuts.
- **[INFERENCE] Not babyish — do:** grade-level vocabulary *with* a one-sentence definition the first time a term appears; real-world, teen-relevant examples (phones, jobs, sports stats, games, money, the news, Iceland-specific contexts); respectful "you're capable" tone; acknowledging difficulty honestly ("Þetta er eitt af erfiðari hugtökunum í 10. bekk."); saying *why* it matters (exams, life).
- **[INFERENCE] Not babyish — don't:** over-praise ("Frábært! Vel gert!" after every prompt), sing-song or exaggerated "kids' presenter" intonation, cartoon sound effects, toddler-style characters, "let's go on an adventure" framing, oversimplified examples that are wrong at grade level, talking down ("þetta er nú bara einfalt").
- **[INFERENCE] "Simple language, not simple ideas":** keep sentence structure short and literal (helps dyslexia and figurative-language weaknesses, which the 2026 study tied to grammar), but keep the conceptual content at the real 10th-grade curriculum level (aðalnámskrá). Short sentences ≠ shallow content.

## 6. Icelandic context

### Takeaway
Iceland has unusually high identified autism prevalence among children in Nordic comparisons (up to ~3.1% of 7–12-year-olds in a 2023 Aarhus report), so an average 10th-grade class is likely to include autistic students. TEACCH-style structured teaching ("skipulögð kennsla") is used in Icelandic schools. I could not retrieve specific teaching guidance from Einhverfusamtökin, RGR or MMS on video/explainer content.

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE]** With ~1–3% identified prevalence plus undiagnosed students, a class of 25 may well include one or more autistic students; designing for them is mainstream, not niche.
