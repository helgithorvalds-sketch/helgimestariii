# How top YouTube math and history explainers are written and structured (for 3–6 min explainers for 10th graders, Icelandic, dyslexia-friendly, narrated with on-screen drawing)

Research date: 20 November 2026. Scope: scripting and storytelling methods of top math and history explainer creators, what makes narration sound human rather than "AI-like", what teens find patronising, and a practical structure.
Evidence labels used below: **[EVIDENCE]** = controlled study / large dataset / survey; **[CREATOR]** = creator's own statement or opinion; **[ANALYSIS]** = third-party commentary; **[INFERENCE]** = my reasoning (no source).

Note on method limits: about 25 searches and fetches. Several primary pages (YouTube itself, pgbovine.net for the Guo et al. PDF) could not be fetched, so **per-video view counts for specific topic videos (Pythagoras, slope, exponents, percentages, probability) could not be verified** and are listed as gaps rather than guessed.

## 1. Math creators: what they do in the first 30 seconds, how they pace worked examples, how they make a formula feel "discovered"

### Takeaway
The best-documented math-explainer principles come from Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown): **concrete before abstract, never start with definitions, visual first, and every on-screen movement must have a purpose**. Sal Khan's model (voice only + hand drawing on a dark background, conversational, pause-able) is the nearest match to "narration with on-screen drawings", and large-scale data shows that hand-drawn "Khan-style" tutorials hold attention better than slides. Popular step-by-step channels (Organic Chemistry Tutor, Math Antics) win on directness and worked examples rather than storytelling, but I could not verify per-video view counts.

### Cited Findings
**3Blue1Brown / Grant Sanderson [CREATOR]**
- Sanderson's advice to explainer makers (from his 2021 Summer of Math Exposition (SoME) video, summarised on his FAQ): "Just start" (iterate and get feedback); **"Concrete before abstract"** (give examples before general frameworks); **"Never start with definitions"** (in math, definitions should be the end point, not the starting point); "Topic choice matters way more than production quality" (but get a good microphone); **"Be visual"** (ask what picture could make the topic clearer); **"Every movement on the screen should be deliberate, with an identifiable purpose"**; be niche. — [3Blue1Brown FAQ](https://www.3blue1brown.com/faq/)
- In the SoME1 results post he names the qualities of excellent math explanation as **"clarity, strong motivation, compelling choices of topic matter, and non-obvious insights"**, and says "As long as it's a solid idea, with an engaging explanation, people will resonate with it"; a piece can stand out because of "a particular visual intuition in the middle" or "an unexpected application of a seemingly useless bit of math." — [3Blue1Brown blog: SoME1 results](https://www.3blue1brown.com/blog/some1-results)
- On scripting: "I do write a script as a guide for what I intend to say, but I don't stick to it exactly... I use it as a loose guide and then make adjustments after seeing the animation." — [Smart Venture Substack interview](https://smartventure.substack.com/p/week-5-here-we-come). He has also said some promised videos never got made because he "spent a good amount of time trying to write a script for it that I just didn't feel was compelling." — [Q&A with Grant Sanderson (rosetta.to transcript)](https://rosetta.to/u/3blue1brown/q-a-with-grant-sanderson-3blue1brown)
- A summary of the SoME announcement video describes his advice as: content over production quality, **structuring explanations from concrete to abstract**, niche topics, and choosing a fitting genre. — [rosetta.to: Make math videos! SoME announcement](https://rosetta.to/u/3blue1brown/make-math-videos-summer-of-math-exposition-announcement)
- He chooses topics by following his own curiosity. — [IAPS interview](https://iaps.info/2025/01/22/exploring-the-path-of-3blue1brown-a-conversation-with-grant-sanderson/)

**Khan Academy / Sal Khan [CREATOR + EVIDENCE]**
- In his TED2011 talk ("Let's use video to reinvent education"), Khan describes the format: "You hear my voice and you see what I am drawing in these kind of pastel, chalk like colors on this black background." — [NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/155228627); [TED talk page](https://www.ted.com/talks/view/id/1090)
- His cousins "preferred me on YouTube than in person". Recorded videos let them "pause and repeat their cousin, without feeling like they're wasting my time" and without embarrassment about re-asking things they "should" already know; "the very last thing you need is another human being saying, 'Do you understand this?'" — [TED 2011 transcript (singjupost)](https://singjupost.com/salman-khan-talk-ted-2011-transcript/)
- A Stanford colloquium abstract (Jan 2011) calls the videos' **"conversational style... the tonal antithesis of what people traditionally associate with math and science instruction."** — [Stanford EE380 abstract](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee380/Abstracts/110126.html)
- **[EVIDENCE]** Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014), "How Video Production Affects Student Engagement" (ACM Learning@Scale), analysed **6.9 million video-watching sessions** across four edX courses. Findings: **shorter videos are more engaging (recommend chunks under 6 minutes)**; **Khan-style tablet-drawing tutorials were more engaging than PowerPoint slides or code screencasts**; mixing a talking head with slides beats slides alone; instructor speaking speed and enthusiasm affect engagement (exact words-per-minute figures not confirmed in what I could read). — [UBC Video Learning Hub summary](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=569); [e-teaching.org summary](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/guo-kim-rubin-2014); [paper PDF (Rochester mirror)](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf). Caveat: these are university MOOC learners, not 15-year-olds.

**Veritasium / Derek Muller: start from the misconception [EVIDENCE + CREATOR]**
- Muller's PhD (University of Sydney, 2008, "Designing Effective Multimedia for Physics Education") found that viewers of clear, purely expository videos **felt** they had learned and grew more confident, but **tests showed little learning**, because the video was absorbed into their existing misconceptions. — [THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx); [Wikipedia: Derek Muller](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek%20Muller)
- Muller, Bewes, Sharma & Reimann (2008), "Saying the wrong thing: improving learning with multimedia by including misconceptions" (*J. Computer Assisted Learning*): **364 first-year physics students were randomly assigned to 4 videos** on Newton's laws: Exposition; Extended Exposition (plus extra interesting material); **Refutation** (a common misconception stated and then refuted); **Dialogue** (student–tutor dialogue around the misconception). The two misconception-based videos produced **significantly more learning** than the two that did not address misconceptions. This held in all three prior-knowledge tracks, with the **largest absolute gains for students with the least prior knowledge**. — [Chris Mead summary](https://meadcj.github.io/posts/2023-11-13-saying-the-wrong-thing.html); [paper abstract summary](https://perticles.blogspot.com/2008/01/muller-sharma-reimann-jcal-2008.html); DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x. Muller links the gain to **greater mental effort** while watching. — [THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx)
- Note that "extra interesting material" alone (Extended Exposition) did **not** match the misconception versions. — [Chris Mead summary](https://meadcj.github.io/posts/2023-11-13-saying-the-wrong-thing.html)
- Veritasium videos typically **start with the common misconception about the topic**. — [THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx)
- Veritasium's "Clickbait is Unreasonably Effective" (28 Nov 2021, 11:21, ~24.1M views per a listing) separates honest "Type 1" clickbait, which briefly states what the video is really about, from deceptive clickbait. — [Recapio digest](https://recapio.com/digest/clickbait-is-unreasonably-effective-by-veritasium); [rosetta.to Veritasium 2021](https://rosetta.to/u/veritasium/2021) (secondary listings; the view count could not be checked on YouTube)

**Eddie Woo (WooTube) [CREATOR + ANALYSIS]**
- Woo frames learning as **"a conversation, not a lecture"** and focuses on making each lesson as good as possible for the students physically in his class, trusting that it carries over to online viewers. — [Google AU blog, World Teachers' Day](https://blog.google/intl/en-au/products/connect-communicate/world-teachers-day/)
- He is described as having "a talent for hooking his students with a great story" and treating technology as "merely an amplifier". — [University of Melbourne, Talking Teaching: Eddie Woo](https://education.unimelb.edu.au/talking-teaching/episodes/eddie-woo)
- He started filming in 2012 for a student who was missing school because of cancer; the videos are unedited real classroom lessons with live student interaction. — [University of Sydney news, 2024](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2024/10/21/teaching-the-teachers.html); [Google AU blog](https://blog.google/intl/en-au/products/connect-communicate/world-teachers-day/)
- Channel size: ~1.92M subscribers and ~178.7M views (Aug 2025, Wikipedia); ~2M subscribers and ~188.7M views (Social Blade, undated capture). — [Wikipedia: Eddie Woo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddie_Woo); [Social Blade](https://socialblade.com/youtube/handle/misterwootube/videos)
- NSW Education (CESE) has short videos of Woo on keeping students engaged and making sure every student is learning. — [NSW CESE: keeping students engaged](https://education.nsw.gov.au/about-us/education-data-and-research/cese/publications/video/keeping-students-engaged-eddie-woo-video.html)

**TED-Ed (math and history lessons) [CREATOR, second-hand]**
- A TED-Ed lesson is a **3–5 minute** animated video (other accounts say **4–7 minutes**), aimed at **ages 13–21**. Writers produce an **"800-900 word script" that is "simple and direct"**. TED-Ed asks writers to **let the animation carry content instead of saying everything aloud** (e.g. show the affected animals on screen rather than listing them). Teachers find this hard because they feel their words "are always super important". Lessons are fact-checked (sources requested), then a narrator and animator are hired. The goal is to "best spark the curiosity of learners". — [EdSurge 2018: Behind the scenes of TED-Ed](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-08-16-behind-the-scenes-of-ted-ed-s-wildly-popular-youtube-channel-for-students); [eSchool News / TED-Ed launch](https://www.eschoolnews.com/district-management/2012/03/23/free-video-lessons-offered-by-leaders-in-innovation-thinking/)

**Math Antics / Organic Chemistry Tutor / Numberphile / Mathologer**
- Math Antics videos are mostly **under 10 minutes**. — [TeachersFirst](https://teachersfirst.com/single.cfm?id=16664). I found no reliable per-video view counts.
- I found no first-hand scripting statements from The Organic Chemistry Tutor, Numberphile or Mathologer within my search budget (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] The first 30 seconds:** across the sourced principles, a strong opening does one of three things: (a) poses a concrete problem or puzzle (Sanderson's "strong motivation", "concrete before abstract"); (b) states a common wrong belief and puts it under pressure (Muller); (c) begins a small story (Woo's "hooking with a story"). None of the sources supports opening with a definition or with "in this video we will learn...", and Sanderson explicitly advises against starting with definitions.
- **[INFERENCE] Making a formula feel discovered:** show 2–3 concrete cases (numbers, drawn shapes), let the viewer see the pattern, and only then write the rule in symbols. The formula comes as a summary ("so every time, we did this... and that's what a² + b² = c² says"), following Sanderson's "definitions are the end point".
- **[INFERENCE] For drawn narration:** the Khan model (voice + live drawing, no face) suits the user's format, and Guo et al. give it empirical support for engagement. Sanderson's "every movement deliberate" maps to "draw only what you are saying right now". TED-Ed's "let the picture carry content" means the script can be **shorter** than the explanation: what is drawn does not also need to be said.
- **[INFERENCE] Word budget:** TED-Ed's 800–900 words for roughly 4–6 minutes is about 150–180 words/min in English. For dyslexic 15-year-olds in Icelandic (longer compound words), a slower pace makes sense: roughly **110–140 words/min, i.e. about 350–450 words for a 3-minute video and 650–800 for 6 minutes**, with pauses while drawing. This is an estimate, not a sourced figure.
- **[INFERENCE] Worked-example pacing (based on the step-by-step genre plus Muller):** state the problem → say what you notice and why you choose this step ("we want c alone, so...") → one step per line drawn → pause or check ("does 25 make sense? c has to be longer than both legs") → name the common mistake (e.g. adding a + b before squaring) and show why it fails.

### Gaps
- **View counts and openings for specific popular videos on Pythagoras, slope/linear functions, exponents, percentages and probability** (Khan, Math Antics, Organic Chemistry Tutor, Eddie Woo, TED-Ed, 3Blue1Brown) could not be verified. YouTube pages were not fetchable, and aggregator data was unreliable. The report writer should not quote numbers for these.
- No first-hand interviews found on scripting by The Organic Chemistry Tutor (Julio Gonzalez), Math Antics (Rob Ellis), Numberphile (Brady Haran) or Mathologer (Burkard Polster).
- The exact speaking-rate and enthusiasm results from Guo et al. (2014) were not readable (the PDF host was blocked in this session).
- No creator-shared audience-retention graphs for math explainers were found.

## 2. History creators: how they turn history into a story (stakes, characters, cause and effect, cliffhangers)

### Takeaway
OverSimplified (9M+ subscribers, ~1.2B views from only ~31 videos) is the clearest proof that **cause → consequence narrative, recurring characters with personalities (countries drawn as blobs with eyes), and jokes placed on a deliberate rhythm** can make history extremely watchable. Crash Course shows that wit and a "snappy pace" work with teens, and TED-Ed shows the same in a 3–5 minute frame. First-hand scripting statements from history creators were scarce in what I found.

### Cited Findings
- **OverSimplified [ANALYSIS]:** 9M+ subscribers, 31 videos, 30 of them with 10–50 million views each, 1.2B+ total views, about 3–4 uploads a year, no shorts and no on-camera presence. Started 2016 with "World War I – Oversimplified (Part 1)", then WWII, Cold War, American Revolution, fall of the Soviet Union. Format: **stick figures on a blank background with a dry voice-over**. Narrative: **opens with story and humour before going deep**; explains wars through **cause and consequence**; **recurring characters, running gags, callbacks** to earlier videos; **deliberate rhythm of jokes, transitions and "moments"**; switches between silly and serious tones; **countries drawn as blobs with eyes** to carry emotion; jokes "disarm viewers and create space for curiosity". — [Async/Podcastle blog: Oversimplified, the faceless YouTuber](https://async.com/blog/who-is-oversimplified/) (third-party analysis, not the creator's own words; figures undated)
- **Crash Course / John Green [ANALYSIS + CREATOR]:** covers heavy history "with wit and humor" and a "snappy pace that feels more like an episode of the Simpsons than the typical classroom lecture"; recurring devices (thought bubbles, the fake fern, letters to "me from the past"). — [Salesforce blog interview](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/interview-with-bestselling-author-john-green-crash-course-in-community-building-and-content-creation/); [Wikipedia: Hank Green](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hank%20Green). Green: Crash Course is meant to **supplement, not replace, textbooks** and can "make fun of" "great man history". — [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/childrens/childrens-authors/article/66676-john-green-to-crash-course-fans-read-your-boring-textbooks.html)
- **John Green on authenticity [CREATOR]:** "Don't be afraid of the medium"; students "are used to bad cuts" and "actually think that a poorly edited video is in some ways cooler or more authentic"; he aims for the feeling "that you're there with [viewers], that you're listening to them". — [EdSurge 2020: How John Green thinks about his educational videos](https://www.edsurge.com/news/2020-04-28-how-youtube-star-john-green-thinks-about-his-educational-videos)
- **TED-Ed history:** chooses curiosity-led angles, e.g. the "most successful pirate of all time"; 4–7 minute lessons; aim to "spark the curiosity of learners". — [EdSurge 2018](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-08-16-behind-the-scenes-of-ted-ed-s-wildly-popular-youtube-channel-for-students)
- **Adjacent explainer creators [CREATOR]:** Tom Scott heavily scripts in his natural speaking voice, researches widely, then condenses into a narrative. — [rosetta.to: How Tom Scott makes videos](https://rosetta.to/u/waveform/how-tom-scott-makes-videos). Joe Hanson (It's Okay to Be Smart) writes "kind of like a sculptor", starting from raw research and cutting down. — [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2017/01/17/joe-hanson-on-communicating-science-on-the-silicon-screen/). Phil Edwards: story and research are "the thing that I'm never going to cheap out on"; makes "the trivial nontrivial". — [The Explainers Substack](https://theexplainers.substack.com/p/making-the-trivialnontrivial-how)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] Transferable to a 3–6 minute school explainer:**
  1. **Start inside a moment, not with a date.** Pick a person or country at a decision point ("1918. Iceland has a choice..."), then go back to explain why.
  2. **Give actors wants and fears.** OverSimplified's countries-with-eyes trick fits on-screen drawing well: draw Denmark, Iceland, Britain as simple characters with expressions. This is cheap, memorable, and helps dyslexic viewers follow who is who without reading names.
  3. **Chain causes explicitly:** "because... so... but then...". This is the "cause and consequence" backbone and replaces lists of facts. Use "but" and "so" transitions rather than "and then".
  4. **Humour about the content itself** (an ironic understatement, a character's reaction), not jokes added on top. In the source description, OverSimplified's jokes come from the historical actors' behaviour.
  5. **Mini cliffhangers between sections** ("This should have ended it. It didn't.") keep momentum in a short video.
  6. **Tone shift for serious moments:** drop the jokes when you reach deaths or injustice. Teens notice when everything is played for laughs.
  7. **Cut hard.** All the sourced creators describe research-then-cut. A 4-minute video carries one causal chain, not a whole chapter.
- **[INFERENCE]** Kurzgesagt, Simple History, Extra History, Historia Civilis and Kings and Generals were in scope, but I found no first-hand scripting material on them within the budget (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No first-hand interview found in which OverSimplified (the creator does not show his face; usually reported as Sam Hall, unconfirmed here) describes his scripting process. The analysis above is third-party.
- No sourced material collected on Simple History, Extra History, Historia Civilis, Kings and Generals or Kurzgesagt scripting methods.
- Per-video lengths and view counts for OverSimplified's top videos were not verified (the source gives only the 10–50M range).

## 3. Writing style: what makes narration sound human and natural rather than robotic or "AI-written"

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is Mayer's **personalization principle**: conversational wording ("you", "we", "let's") beats formal wording on transfer tests (median d of about 0.8–1.1 in Mayer's reviews; smaller, medium-sized effects in an independent meta-analysis). This holds especially for beginners and when it is **not overdone**. Creator advice adds: write in your own speaking voice, speak to one listener as in a conversation, let the visuals carry what does not need to be said, and accept imperfection over polish.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE] Personalization principle (Mayer):** people learn better when multimedia words are **conversational rather than formal**. Mayer's chapter reports conversational wording beating formal in **11/11 tests, median d = 1.11**; a later review reports **14/17 tests, median d = 0.79**; Mayer's 2025 slides list **13/15, median d = 1.00**. — [Cambridge: Personalization, voice and image principles](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/personalization-voice-and-image-principles/97F9B31362E6491806A4718FECCADE3D); [Mayer slides, Media & Learning 2025](https://media-and-learning.eu/files/2025/07/18June_Auditorium_Mayer.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] Independent check:** Ginns, Martin & Marsh (2013) meta-analysis found a **medium effect on transfer and a small effect on retention**. Boundary conditions: most effective when **not overdone** and for **beginners**; may not hold for high achievers or long lessons. A five-experiment Czech study found **no overall advantage**, possibly a cultural boundary condition. — [Almeida et al. 2024, J. Computer Assisted Learning (PDF)](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/67483/1/Computer%20Assisted%20Learning%20-%202024%20-%20Almeida%20-%20More%20personal%20but%20not%20better%20The%20personalization%20effect%20in%20learning%20neutral.pdf); [Czech personalization study (PDF)](https://artemis.ms.mff.cuni.cz/main/papers/personaliz_manuscript_161226_CE_for_web.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] Voice and emotion:** learners can recognise an instructor's emotional tone from voice alone, and the "positivity principle" finds better responses and learning when the instructor shows positive emotion (college samples). — [Lawson et al. 2021 (Purdue PDF)](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Lawson2021EdTechResDev.pdf); [Springer: s11423-021-10057-w](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-021-10057-w)
- **[EVIDENCE] Misconception "dialogue" format:** a student–tutor dialogue that voices the wrong idea and resolves it outperformed plain exposition. — [Chris Mead summary of Muller et al. 2008](https://meadcj.github.io/posts/2023-11-13-saying-the-wrong-thing.html)
- **[CREATOR]** Eddie Woo: learning should feel like "a conversation, not a lecture". — [Google AU blog](https://blog.google/intl/en-au/products/connect-communicate/world-teachers-day/)
- **[CREATOR]** Tom Scott writes scripts **in his natural voice**. — [rosetta.to: How Tom Scott makes videos](https://rosetta.to/u/waveform/how-tom-scott-makes-videos)
- **[CREATOR]** Sanderson treats the script as "a loose guide" and adjusts after seeing the visuals. — [Smart Venture Substack](https://smartventure.substack.com/p/week-5-here-we-come)
- **[CREATOR]** TED-Ed: "simple and direct", and do not narrate what the picture already shows. — [EdSurge 2018](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-08-16-behind-the-scenes-of-ted-ed-s-wildly-popular-youtube-channel-for-students)
- **[CREATOR]** John Green: imperfection reads as authentic to students. — [EdSurge 2020](https://www.edsurge.com/news/2020-04-28-how-youtube-star-john-green-thinks-about-his-educational-videos)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] Concrete anti-"AI-sound" checklist** (derived from the above, adapted for Icelandic):
  - **Say it aloud before keeping it.** If you would not say a sentence to a 15-year-old across a table, rewrite it (natural-voice principle). Read the script aloud or record a rough take, then transcribe what you actually said.
  - **Cut announcement phrases:** "Í þessu myndbandi ætlum við að læra...", "Mikilvægt er að hafa í huga að...", "Í stuttu máli sagt...", and summary-of-summary endings. Start with the problem.
  - **Avoid AI tells:** groups of three adjectives, "ekki bara X heldur líka Y", rhetorical "En hvað þýðir það í raun?" after every paragraph, uniformly long, balanced sentences, and over-praise ("frábær spurning!", "þetta er ótrúlega spennandi!"). Vary sentence length; keep many sentences short.
  - **Use the spoken register of Icelandic:** "við" and "þú", everyday verbs (fá, setja, taka, finna), and spoken connectors ("sko" sparingly, "jæja", "allt í lagi", "bíddu", "sjáðu"), not written-register connectors ("enn fremur", "þar af leiðandi", "í ljósi þess").
  - **Admit the confusing part.** "Þetta ruglar flesta: ..." is the Muller refutation move, and it shows respect.
  - **One idea per sentence; name the thing, then use the same word every time.** Do not switch between synonyms (for dyslexic viewers and second-language readers, consistent terms reduce load).
  - **Jokes about the content, not added on.** Small, dry and optional, never "fun!" signposting.
  - **Do not over-explain the obvious.** If it is drawn on screen, do not also describe it (TED-Ed rule).
  - **Do not overdo personalization** (boundary condition): one "þú" sentence per beat is enough; constant "vá, við erum að fara að..." sounds childish.

### Gaps
- No specific published analysis was found of what makes narration sound "AI-generated" to viewers; the checklist above is inference.
- No Icelandic-language studies on conversational vs formal instructional register were found. The Czech null result suggests the effect may vary by language and culture.

## 4. Teen audience: what 15–16-year-olds find patronising, and their preferences for educational video

### Takeaway
Teens are heavy YouTube users (Pew: 90% use it, 73% daily), and a large and rising share use video for schoolwork (Ofcom: 48% of UK 3–17s in 2024). I found **no study that directly measures what teens find patronising in educational video**. The nearest evidence: condescension in feedback is disliked, controlling tone provokes resistance, short videos hold attention better, and Khan's cousins valued private, pause-able, non-judgmental video. Most length evidence comes from university samples.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE] Pew (US, 1,391 teens aged 13–17, Sept–Oct 2024, published 12 Dec 2024):** 90% use YouTube (95% in 2022); **73% use it daily**; 15% "almost constantly". YouTube is the top platform. — [Pew Research: Teens, Social Media and Technology 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/12/12/teens-social-media-and-technology-2024/)
- **[EVIDENCE] Ofcom (UK):** YouTube is the most-used app or site across all children's age groups (~88%). **48% of children aged 3–17 watched videos that help with schoolwork or learning in 2024, up from 42% in 2023.** The Children's Media Lives qualitative study found digital platforms built into most children's learning, and many found digital learning easier to organise. — [Ofcom Children's Media Literacy Report 2025 (PDF)](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/media-literacy-research/children/childrens-media-use-and-attitudes-report-2025/childrens-media-literacy-report-2025.pdf); [Ofcom Children's Media Literacy Report 2024 (PDF)](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0025/283048/Childrens-Media-Literacy-Report-2024.pdf); [Teaching Times summary of Children's Media Lives 2024](https://teachingtimes.com/childrens-media-lives-2024). Ofcom reporting also notes children are drawn to "dramatic, short-form videos". — [Prolific North](https://www.prolificnorth.co.uk/?p=74646)
- **[EVIDENCE] Secondary math:** an Italian study of secondary students found they use YouTube as their **main resource for consultation and help in understanding mathematics**. — [Papeles (UAN) article PDF](https://revistas.uan.edu.co/index.php/papeles/article/download/1431/1167/6056)
- **[EVIDENCE] Length:** shorter tutorials raised engagement (Doolittle et al., 212 undergraduates); length did not correlate with likes or dislikes (Shoufan & Mohamed); engagement falls as math videos get longer (Portuguese higher-ed study). **Contradicted by** one study where students preferred 10–15 minutes over the commonly recommended ~6. A German/English review notes there is **no comprehensive empirical study of ideal educational video length**. — [ULPGC comparison paper (PDF)](https://accedacris.ulpgc.es/bitstream/10553/128990/1/comparing_educational_dissemination_videos.pdf); [IJIET V13N10 (PDF)](https://www.ijiet.org/vol13/IJIET-V13N10-1955.pdf); [Springer s10758-024-09745-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10758-024-09745-2). Guo et al. (6.9M sessions) recommend **under 6 minutes**. — [UBC summary](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=569)
- **[EVIDENCE, adjacent] Condescension:** a 2025 study of 148 undergraduates found students react poorly to feedback seen as "rude, sarcastic, vague, overly directive, or patronizing"; respectful feedback avoids any implication that the student is intellectually inferior. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1585447/full). Controlling language or a harsh tone provokes "defiant, oppositional reactions". — [PMC5998313](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5998313/)
- **[CREATOR] Private, pause-able learning:** Khan's point that video lets learners re-watch "without feeling like they're wasting my time" or being asked "Do you understand this?". — [TED 2011 transcript](https://singjupost.com/salman-khan-talk-ted-2011-transcript/)
- **[CREATOR] Teens accept rough edges:** John Green says students find poorly edited video "cooler or more authentic". — [EdSurge 2020](https://www.edsurge.com/news/2020-04-28-how-youtube-star-john-green-thinks-about-his-educational-videos)
- **[CREATOR] Target age:** TED-Ed aims at 13–21, which matches 15–16. — [EdSurge 2018](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-08-16-behind-the-scenes-of-ted-ed-s-wildly-popular-youtube-channel-for-students)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE] What likely reads as patronising to 15–16-year-olds** (combining the condescension evidence, the "not overdone" personalization caveat, and the success of dry, deadpan creators such as OverSimplified, Crash Course and 3Blue1Brown): exaggerated enthusiasm ("Vá! Stærðfræði er svo skemmtileg!"); praising the viewer for trivial steps; cartoonish analogies meant for children (pizza slices for 10th-grade percentages); explaining what they obviously know; "kids" framing ("krakkar"); moralising endings; fake slang. Respectful alternatives: deadpan tone, real-world stakes (money, phones, sport, games, loans/interest, sale prices), naming the hard part honestly, and treating viewers as capable.
- **[INFERENCE] Simple vocabulary is not childish content.** The feedback ("simple words, real 10th-grade level") matches the Khan and TED-Ed model: plain, direct sentences with full-level ideas. Keep proper terms (e.g. "veldi", "hallatala", "líkur") but introduce each once with a picture, then reuse it consistently.
- **[INFERENCE] Dyslexia link:** a voice-led video with drawing removes reading load. Keep on-screen text to key terms and numbers, write slowly as you say them, and allow pause points. Khan's pause-and-repeat argument applies strongly here.

### Gaps
- **No direct study of what 15–16-year-olds find patronising in educational video** was found. The evidence above is adjacent (undergraduates, written feedback).
- Pew's 2024 report did not give a "teens use YouTube to learn" figure in what I retrieved; the Ofcom 48% is for ages 3–17 and is not broken out for 15–16.
- No Icelandic data (e.g. from Menntamálastofnun or Fjölmiðlanefnd) on teens' use of educational video was searched or found.
- No research found specifically on dyslexic teens and explainer-video pacing.

## 5. Practical structure: a synthesised template for a 3–8 minute teen math (and history) explainer

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a **short (under ~6 min), single-idea, drawn, conversational** video that opens with a concrete problem or a common misconception, builds intuition with concrete cases before the rule, works one textbook-style example step by step, explicitly refutes the common mistake, and ends with a problem for the viewer to try. No creator-shared retention analytics were found to tune the timings, so the timings below are inference.

### Cited Findings (evidence behind each element)
- Under 6 minutes, chunked: [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014 via UBC](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=569). TED-Ed 3–5 or 4–7 min: [EdSurge 2018](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-08-16-behind-the-scenes-of-ted-ed-s-wildly-popular-youtube-channel-for-students)
- Hand-drawn tutorial style is more engaging than slides: [Guo et al. via UBC](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=569)
- Concrete before abstract; never start with definitions; be visual; deliberate motion: [3Blue1Brown FAQ](https://www.3blue1brown.com/faq/)
- Strong motivation and non-obvious insight: [3Blue1Brown SoME1 results](https://www.3blue1brown.com/blog/some1-results)
- State and refute the misconception (refutation or dialogue): [Muller et al. 2008 summary](https://meadcj.github.io/posts/2023-11-13-saying-the-wrong-thing.html)
- Conversational wording, not overdone: [Mayer, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/personalization-voice-and-image-principles/97F9B31362E6491806A4718FECCADE3D); [Almeida et al. 2024](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/67483/1/Computer%20Assisted%20Learning%20-%202024%20-%20Almeida%20-%20More%20personal%20but%20not%20better%20The%20personalization%20effect%20in%20learning%20neutral.pdf)
- Let the visual carry content; ~800–900 words max: [EdSurge 2018](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-08-16-behind-the-scenes-of-ted-ed-s-wildly-popular-youtube-channel-for-students)
- History: cause → consequence, characters, rhythm of jokes, serious/silly shifts: [Async blog on OverSimplified](https://async.com/blog/who-is-oversimplified/)

### Inferences
**[INFERENCE] Math template (about 4–5 min, about 500–700 Icelandic words):**
1. **Hook, 0:00–0:25:** a concrete problem a 15-year-old could meet ("Sjónvarpið er auglýst 55 tommu. Kemst það í hilluna þína?" for Pythagoras; "Úlpan var lækkuð um 30% og svo aftur um 20%. Er það 50% afsláttur?" for percentages). Or state the common wrong belief ("Flestir halda að... "). No greeting, no "í þessu myndbandi".
2. **Intuition / visual, 0:25–1:30:** two or three concrete cases drawn live; the viewer sees the pattern before it has a name.
3. **The rule, 1:30–2:00:** now name it and write it as the summary of what we just saw. Say the term once clearly, draw it, then reuse the same word.
4. **Worked example, 2:00–3:30:** a typical textbook problem at real 10th-grade level. One step per line; say *why* each step ("við viljum fá x eitt og sér, svo..."); one sanity check ("passar þetta? lengsta hliðin verður að vera lengst").
5. **Common mistake, 3:30–4:10:** show the wrong method, let it look tempting, then show where it breaks (Muller refutation). Optionally voice it as a student ("En af hverju má ég ekki bara leggja saman?").
6. **Your turn, 4:10–4:40:** one problem on screen, "pásaðu og prófaðu", then the answer shown briefly. End there; no "takk fyrir að horfa, vonandi lærðirðu eitthvað".

**[INFERENCE] History template (about 4–6 min):** cold open in a moment of decision → who wants what (characters drawn with faces) → cause chain ("af því að... þannig að... en þá...") with one mini-cliffhanger in the middle → the turning point → consequence for today or for Iceland → one sharp question to think about. Humour comes from the actors' behaviour; drop it at serious moments.

**[INFERENCE] Script hygiene for this project:** write → read aloud → record a rough take → cut every sentence that the drawing already shows → cut every announcement phrase → check that each technical term is used the same way every time → have a real 15-year-old (ideally one with dyslexia) watch it and say where they drifted.

### Gaps
- **No audience-retention data shared by these creators** was found (e.g. where viewers drop off in math explainers). Veritasium has discussed titles/thumbnails ([rosetta.to: My Video Went Viral](https://rosetta.to/u/veritasium/my-video-went-viral-here-s-why)), but I did not retrieve retention specifics.
- The section timings and word counts above are proposals derived from the sources, not measured optima.
- No study directly compares this structure with alternatives for 15–16-year-olds.
